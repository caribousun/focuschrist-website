A parable taught by Jesus Christ

The Prodigal Son and His Brother

One son comes home from far away; the other stands outside the celebration. The father goes toward both, and the story leaves an invitation before us.

Read and discover

A father and two sons

Jesus tells of a father with two sons after people criticize His welcome of sinners. That opening context matters throughout the account. The story includes the son who leaves, but it also includes a son who remains near home and struggles to share the father's joy. Luke 15:1-32

Luke 15:1-32

Read and discover

The road away and the road home

The younger son asks for his portion of the property, leaves for a distant country, and wastes what he has. When a famine comes, he is in need. He takes work feeding swine and longs for food, yet no one gives him what he needs.

In that low place he remembers that his father's hired servants have bread. He resolves to return, acknowledge his sin, and ask for the place of a servant. The movement toward home includes a changed recognition of his condition and a willingness to speak truthfully.

Luke 15:1-32

Read and discover

Welcome and the brother outside

While the son is still far away, the father sees him and runs to meet him. He embraces and kisses him. The son begins his confession, and the father calls for a robe, a ring, shoes, and a feast. The welcome is not delayed until the son has repaid the lost inheritance.

The elder son hears music and learns why there is a celebration. Angry, he refuses to enter. The father goes out to him as well. Their conversation reveals the elder son's sense that long service has gone unrecognized and that his brother's welcome diminishes his own place.

Luke 15:1-32

Read and discover

The invitation remains open

The father affirms the elder son's place with him while insisting that the brother's return is reason for joy. The story ends without telling us whether the elder son enters. We should not invent a final embrace or use the open ending to announce that he is permanently beyond mercy.

Both conversations can invite honest reflection. Some readers may recognize the difficulty of coming home with failure to acknowledge; others may recognize resentment when mercy seems more generous than their accounting allows. Neither recognition requires pretending that real family wounds resolve as quickly as a story is read.

You might pause with the father's movement toward each son: running to the returning one and going out to the one who will not enter. The invitation to reconciliation does not erase agency. The account leaves room to ask what receiving mercy and rejoicing in another person's return might require of you.

Pause and consider

  • Which son's struggle is harder for you to understand?
  • How might you receive mercy and also rejoice when another receives it?
