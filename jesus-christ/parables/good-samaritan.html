A parable taught by Jesus Christ

The Good Samaritan

A question about who counts as a neighbor becomes a story about acting as one. Follow the mercy that begins on the road and continues at the inn.

The question about a neighbor

A lawyer asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus directs him to the law, and the reply joins love of God with love of neighbor. When the man then asks who his neighbor is, Jesus answers with a traveler on the road from Jerusalem to Jericho. Luke 10:25-37

Luke 10:25-37

Read and discover

Three travelers encounter a need

Robbers strip and beat the traveler, leaving him half dead. A priest sees him and passes on the other side. A Levite also sees him and passes by. Jesus records their actions without explaining their private reasons; a careful retelling should not invent motives for either.

A Samaritan comes near, sees the wounded man, and is moved with compassion. That compassion becomes practical work. He binds the wounds, uses oil and wine, places the man on his own beast, and takes him to an inn.

Luke 10:25-37

Read and discover

Mercy that continues beyond the road

The care continues after the first emergency. The Samaritan tends the traveler, gives money to the host, and undertakes further expense if needed. Mercy in the story includes interruption, transport, provision, and follow-through.

Jesus' final question changes the direction of the lawyer's original question. Rather than drawing a boundary around who qualifies for help, He asks which person became a neighbor to the wounded man. The lawyer identifies the one who showed mercy, and Jesus calls him to act likewise.

Luke 10:25-37

Read and discover

Becoming a neighbor

The contrast with a Samaritan would matter to the story's original hearers, but we should not turn that contrast into caricatures of whole communities. Nor should we identify the unnamed traveler or the innkeeper as historical people whose biographies are known.

As you reflect, notice both the first moment of stopping and the later work of sustaining care. A person may need more than an expression of sympathy. What responsible, practical help can you offer, and when should you involve others who can provide the care you cannot?

Pause and consider

  • What makes stopping costly for you?
  • What practical follow-through could turn concern into care?
