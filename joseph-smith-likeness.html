02 · What the plaster preserves

The death masks

Historical artifact photograph

Joseph on the left. Hyrum on the right.

BYU’s catalogue identifies Joseph on the left and Hyrum on the right. This photograph lets us study the surviving casts without treating their worn surfaces as an unchanged view of living faces.

Courtesy of LDS Church Archives; BYU item 4109, catalogue date 2001, designated public domain.

Explore the mask collection and 3D models ↗

The museum dates George Cannon’s molds to June 29, 1844, after the brothers’ deaths. The date when the surviving casts were made is unknown. Their forms offer important evidence of facial proportions, while repairs and copying complicate individual details.

Explore the evidence: casts, copies, and 3D models

The museum distinguishes the Dibble masks from the Pedestal masks, whose history is less certain. Some details differ because of repair or finishing. Treat each object as a source with its own history.

Church History specialists later used photographs from many angles to create detailed 3D models of the surviving masks. You can examine those artifacts through the official downloads. A model of a cast does not supply a living expression, eye color, or hair.

03 · A moment held by an artist

Joseph sat for a portrait

Joseph’s journal records a sitting for a profile on June 25, 1842. The Joseph Smith Papers identifies the artist as Sutcliffe Maudsley. A portrait made from life is valuable evidence, but its line, angle, and style still deserve attention. Read the entry and its annotation.

04 · From references to a consistent likeness

How we developed the focusChrist portrait

Our portrait was developed using the Church History Museum’s death-mask references. Joseph’s masks informed the facial proportions; Hyrum’s were considered for comparison. We adopted the resulting artistic portrait as the reference for new scenes, keeping the same recognizable face as pose and expression change.

The research now follows the portrait feature by feature, from the shape preserved in the masks to written descriptions of hair, eyes and complexion. It also separates the documented creation of our image from the historical review that followed.

Portrait Research
Explore our process: evidence, interpretation, and review
  1. Begin with the references. The masks inform structure. Historical portraits and descriptions help readers examine appearance from other perspectives.
  2. Make the creative choices visible. Living eyes, skin, hair, expression, clothing, and light require interpretation. The adopted portrait is not an authenticated photograph or a forensic identification.
  3. Keep the person recognizable. New scenes use the adopted portrait as their image reference. We compare faces, hands, expression, clothing, and the full composition, including how the picture reads on a phone.

This page’s artwork follows that process. Our project record establishes how we developed and adopted the image; it does not establish historical certainty about every feature.

18 January 1827 · Joseph 21, Emma 22

Choosing a life together

Joseph and Emma married in South Bainbridge, New York. Joseph’s history records their marriage despite opposition from Emma’s family, then their move to his parents’ home. Their partnership began before the translation and publication of the Book of Mormon.

Consider: What does it mean to begin a shared life without knowing all it will require?

Harmony · autumn 1828

A small gift amid demanding work

The Church’s home history recounts Joseph earning three dollars for four days of work and spending twenty-five cents on a comb for Emma. A modest purchase brings their household concerns into view.

Consider: Which small act of thoughtfulness could express care in your home?

Harmony · early 1828

Emma’s work as a scribe

Emma helped Joseph as an early scribe. Martin Harris took over that role in April 1828. Her writing belongs within the history of the translation, as well as the history of their marriage.

Emma was expecting their first child during this early scribal work.

Consider: Whose quiet work helps make a larger undertaking possible?

June 1844 · Hyrum 44

Hyrum reflects beside scripture

Doctrine and Covenants 135 remembers Hyrum reading near the close of Ether 12 on the morning he prepared to leave for Carthage. He marked the passage by turning down the page. Its words of charity, faithfulness and strength in weakness place his final journey within his devotion to Jesus Christ.

Consider: What passage of scripture helps you remain faithful when the way ahead is uncertain?

Nauvoo · 17 March 1842

Seeking out those in need

At the first meeting of the Female Relief Society of Nauvoo, Emma was elected president. After the men withdrew, she urged the women to find and relieve people in distress, do good, and care for one another’s character and reputation. The minutes preserve leadership expressed through practical service.

She undertook this service about six weeks after giving birth to a son who did not survive. The meeting record’s historical notes preserve this recent loss alongside her public work.

Consider: Who nearby needs to be noticed, treated fairly, or helped?

Harmony · June 1828

Staying close in sorrow

Their first child died shortly after his birth on 15 June 1828. Emma became seriously ill, and Joseph cared for her through the following two weeks.

Consider: How can steady presence help when words cannot remove another person’s grief?

November 1838 · Emma 34

Love carried across separation

On 12 November 1838, Joseph wrote to Emma while held in Richmond, Missouri. He expressed affection for her, addressed their children, and hoped for their reunion. His invitation to visit left room for Emma’s own judgment. The letter makes the family’s separation personal.

Their youngest living child, Alexander, had been born that June. Concern for the children formed part of the separation these letters sought to bridge.

Consider: What can a thoughtful letter offer someone you cannot be with?

Harmony · autumn 1828

Work that sustained a household

Joseph supported the household through hired farm labor, including hauling wood. The ledger places this work alongside the demands of translation and family life.

Consider: How can necessary everyday work become an expression of love?

Early December 1827 · Joseph 21, Emma 23

Making a new home

Joseph and Emma arrived in Pennsylvania’s Susquehanna Valley in early December 1827. They moved toward Emma’s family while seeking a place to live, work and continue the translation. Historical accounts also credit her brother Alva with helping the move.

Emma was expecting their first child when they arrived in Pennsylvania.

Consider: What help makes a difficult move or new beginning possible?

Late June 1830 · Emma 25

Praying together through uncertainty

Emma was baptized on 28 June 1830, but Joseph’s arrest disrupted the planned confirmation meeting. While he faced custody and court proceedings, she joined other women in prayer. Their shared worship offered a way to turn toward God amid anxiety.

Consider: Who could you join in prayer when someone is facing uncertainty?

Forgiveness and remembrance

In July 1840, William W. Phelps sought reconciliation after his break with the Church. Joseph’s reply welcomed his return and expressed joy in renewed friendship. The surviving letterbook copy preserves that act of forgiveness.

After Joseph and Hyrum were killed, Phelps’s poem, now known as “Praise to the Man,” appeared in the Times and Seasons on 1 August 1844. It joined remembrance of Joseph’s ministry with hope in his work beyond death. Love and gratitude can inspire careful study: honoring a person also means being truthful about what the record preserves.

The original letter to Phelps is not extant; a contemporary letterbook copy survives. The hymn’s publication is distinct from an undocumented scene of its composition.
