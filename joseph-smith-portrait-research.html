Chapter 01

A face encountered through history

A portrait informed by history

How did we arrive at our Joseph? Let’s bring the pictures and the written accounts together and look at them one at a time. We have portraits connected to sittings in Nauvoo, visitors’ descriptions, and the contours preserved in plaster after his death. Each gives us something different to work with. Together, they help us understand the face you see here.

Begin with the people who saw him

First, let’s examine the casts that preserve his contours, then meet the artists who saw Joseph in life. Then we’ll listen to visitors and friends. As we move through the face, feature by feature, keep our portrait beside those sources. We’ll finish by bringing that evidence together with the surviving account of how our Joseph was made.

Notice how angle and light change our view of the face; keep the cast’s condition in mind.

Chapter 02

A guide to this journey

Look, read, and return to the source

We can take this slowly. Look at the shape of a nose in profile, the closed lids of a cast, or a word on a printed page. Numbered references lead to the source notes; links beside illustrations open their fuller context. A letter written soon after a meeting and a memory published decades later can both matter, but they reach us by different paths.

Chapter 03

The artists and the casts

Let’s begin with what these objects preserve. On 29 June 1844, molds were taken of Joseph and Hyrum Smith’s faces. The surviving casts preserve a physical connection to their facial contours. Their own histories matter: the original molding date is known, while the manufacture dates of surviving copies are less certain. Repairs, missing areas, copying, and changes after death all affect what we see. Several casts descended from one mold do not become several independent witnesses. 1

The photograph reproduced here comes from BYU's catalogue, which identifies Joseph on the left and Hyrum on the right. It lets us examine the surviving surfaces together. This photograph entered the present research later than the portrait's creation; its value here is as a window onto the objects and their condition. mask comparison in the portrait study · 1 · 20

A profile sitting in Nauvoo

Now turn to a picture made during Joseph’s life. He sat for a profile on 25 June 1842, two years before the molds were taken. The journal gives us that encounter with an artist; the surviving pictures require a further journey through their object histories. The museum identifies the original sitting drawing as still unlocated. Hatch describes a painting rediscovered in 1982 as the probable source for a map image. That painting, its later copies, and the sitting drawing should be kept distinct. The resulting profile tradition is especially useful for studying silhouette. 2 · 3 · 5 · 23

A face painted from life

Next, look at the face from the front. September 1842 journal entries bring us to further painting sessions and the familiar oil attributed to David Rogers. Its frontal view offers another way of seeing Joseph. Curator Robert O. Davis argues that this painting deserves greater weight than Hatch allowed, questioning comparisons that depend on scaling and camera angle. Their disagreement reminds us to look closely at how a comparison was made, as well as at the pictures themselves. 4 · 5 · 22

Chapter 04

Descriptions in their own time

For color, expression, and the impression Joseph made in person, we need to turn from objects to people. Let’s read what visitors and friends noticed. We’ll keep two dates in mind as we go: when they saw him, and when their words reached the page.

Visitors write home

In February 1840, Matthew Davis thought Joseph looked forty to forty-five. Joseph was thirty-four. Three years later, the Taggart letter described a young-looking man who was easy to like. Their differing impressions bring us closer to the experience of meeting him: age appeared through manner, expression, and the expectations of the visitor. Quincy supplies another sharply dated glimpse. His letter of 16 May 1844 describes several days of beard growth and the clothes Joseph wore during the visit. 7 6 8

Quincy returns to the encounter

Decades later, Quincy returned to Joseph in the recollections published in 1881–1883. The familiar descriptions of forehead, nose, and eyes belong to that later telling. The contemporary letter and the memoir let us see both the immediacy of a visit and the shape it took in memory. Reading them together preserves the value of each. 8

These pages identify the publication and its description. The publication date is separate from the remembered event and from any earlier letter.

The memories of those who knew him

Pratt's autobiography remembers appearance alongside manner: hair, complexion, build, grooming, and an affable presence. First published in 1874 after his death, it reached readers through editing. Lydia Knight's history, published in 1883 as a later biography, carries another remembered encounter. These accounts help us imagine how Joseph appeared to people, while their publication histories show how those memories came down to us. 9 10

A newspaper description survives in a later book

A letter dated 26 April 1844, attributed to the Saint Louis Weekly Gazette issue of 18 May, gives unusually particular details: light hazel eyes, fine hair, and conspicuous light lashes. The page shown here comes from its 1916 transcription in The Birth of Mormonism. The original newspaper issue has not been inspected. The description's specificity makes it worth reading closely, including the author's judgments alongside the physical observations. 11

Haven notices blue eyes

Charlotte Haven's letter dated 22 January 1843 offers another vivid impression: blue eyes, light hair and complexion, a youthful appearance, stout build, and round shoulders. The letter was published in the December 1890 Overland Monthly. The surviving printed page gives us these words; Haven's autograph and its editorial path into print remain to be examined. Her blue-eye description stands beside the Gazette's hazel, inviting comparison rather than an easy vote. 21

These sources do not form a simple vote. Independence, record date, editing and transmission matter more than the raw number of quotations. The conflict between hazel and blue therefore remains explicit.

Chapter 05

The shape of the face

Look closely · Follow the evidence

A long oval face

Let’s start with the whole outline of our Joseph. The upper face is broad and the contour narrows toward the chin. Joseph's cast gives us a physical contour to examine, while the Gazette description, preserved in a later transcription, calls his head an oblong oval. Together they draw attention to the same broad shape, without supplying a precise width-to-length ratio. 1 11

Look closely · Follow the evidence

The forehead in changing views

Move up to the forehead. It is broad, with fine lines above a substantial brow. A cast records surface contour; a profile reveals slope. Quincy's later memoir adds a verbal impression of a retreating forehead. Looking from one view to another shows why a small change of angle can alter the apparent shape. 1 3 8

Look closely · Follow the evidence

A prominent nose

Now look at the feature that gives the face its strong center: a long bridge, rounded tip, and visible breadth around the nostrils. Compare the cast with the profile engraving. They bring out different aspects of this feature, filtered through the cast's condition, the photograph's light, and the artist's and engraver's work. 1 3 5

Look closely · Follow the evidence

The mouth and the space above it

Look closely at the mouth. Two details are easy to confuse: the skin between nose and mouth, and the red part of the upper lip. The portrait has a moderate upper lip and a fuller lower one. The Gazette transcription describes thin lips, so our fuller lower lip differs from that observer’s words. Looking separately at the lip and the space above it helps us see exactly where the comparison agrees or differs. 11

Chapter 06

The chin and the living eyes

Look closely · Follow the evidence

The chin completes the outline

Follow the outline down. The lower face ends in a firm chin with rounded-square corners. The cast offers a useful reference for that contour, though damage, incomplete coverage, and the angle of the photograph affect what can be compared. Words such as round or square describe an impression rather than a measured width. 1

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Blue eyes, hazel eyes, and two printed pages

Now we reach a question the cast cannot answer: what color were his eyes? In our Joseph, they appear blue-gray. Haven's letter, dated January 1843 and published in 1890, describes blue eyes. An April 26, 1844 letter attributed to the May 18 Gazette, preserved here in a 1916 transcription, describes light hazel eyes. The disagreement deserves to remain visible: neither plaster nor monochrome art can settle color, and the exact shade remains unknown. 8 9 10 11 12 21

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Opening the eyes

Look beyond color to the shape of the eyes. Here they are open and attentive, with folds below the lids and small differences between the two sides. The cast preserves closed lids. Haven's printed account adds an impression of eyes set back in the head. Living eye opening, head angle, and illumination all contribute to the depth we perceive. 1 21

Look closely · Follow the evidence

The small detail of lashes

For the brows and lashes, let’s return to the Gazette transcription. It draws unusual attention to long, light lashes and light, thick brows. In the portrait the brows are substantial and the lashes quieter. The words invite us to notice this small feature, though they cannot tell us the number, length, or precise color of the hairs. 11

Chapter 07

Hair, skin, and changing appearance

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Hair seen in light

Look at the lighter strands running through our Joseph’s brown hair. Pratt's recollection and Haven's published letter both describe light hair; other accounts and later summaries suggest a range around lighter brown and dark blond. Such words leave room for differences in illumination, age, and the vocabulary of the observer. 9 11 12 21

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Texture is not the same as hairstyle

Now follow the sweep of the hair. It has visible wave and volume. Descriptions of fine or straight hair concern its natural qualities, while a portrait also shows arrangement. An engraved wave does not necessarily mean natural curl. Here the fuller arrangement gives the head a distinct silhouette. 12

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Warmth in the complexion

Turn from the hair to the cheeks. The skin is fair, with warm cheeks, fine texture, folds, and freckle-like marks. Descriptions of a light complexion support the broad palette. They do not preserve each small mark or the amount of redness in a particular moment. The cast offers contour, while the written accounts help us think about living color. 9 10 11 12

Look closely · Follow the evidence

A face between shaves

Our principal portrait is clean-shaven. Let’s set it beside what the visitors remembered. Pratt remembered very little beard; Quincy's contemporary account of his May 1844 visit describes several days of growth. These observations can belong to the same man at different moments. The later Quincy memoir shown nearby should be read alongside, rather than substituted for, the contemporary letter. 8 9

Chapter 08

The presence of a living person

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Thirty-eight, and seen differently

How old does he seem to you? Joseph was thirty-eight when he died in June 1844. In 1840 Davis estimated an older man, while Taggart and Haven described a youthful impression. A birth date fixes age; an encounter tells us how age seemed to another person. The portrait presents a mature man near the end of his thirties. 6 7 21

Look closely · Follow the evidence

An expression among many

Watch how the eyes and mouth work together. The closed mouth and attentive gaze give this portrait a quiet expression. Pratt remembered an affable manner and an unforced smile; Taggart wrote of the man he encountered with warmth. Those accounts suggest a changing, responsive person. A single portrait can offer one expression from that fuller life. 6 9

Look closely · Follow the evidence

The individuality of unequal contours

Compare the two sides of our Joseph, especially the brows, lids, and corners of the mouth: they do not mirror one another perfectly. Those differences make the face more particular. No reliable source examined here establishes a prominent diagnostic facial scar, and an uneven cast surface should not be mistaken for a living injury.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

Beyond the edge of the bust

Finally, look at what frames the face. The dark coat and white neckcloth frame a portrait of the head and shoulders. Descriptions of Joseph's stature and strength refer to his whole body, which this view does not show. Quincy's clothing observations belong to a particular visit. They remind us that one man's wardrobe and appearance could change with his work and occasion. 8 9

Chapter 09

From historical reference to a living portrait

Where we began

We’ve looked at the features separately. Now let’s return to how we brought our Joseph together. We began with the mask references: Joseph’s for the primary proportions, with Hyrum’s alongside them for comparison. Those are the reference roles named in the surviving account of the work. 27

On 14 September 2026, this became the Joseph we would carry into later scenes. The complete original prompt and intermediate measurement notes have not been recovered, so we can follow the surviving reference account and the finished face, rather than every step between them. 27

Put the cast beside our portrait and notice how much changes when the face comes to life. The plaster leaves some choices open. Open eyes need color and expression. Skin needs warmth, hair needs arrangement, and clothing and light give the figure a setting. Historical descriptions illuminate some of these choices. Joseph and Hyrum remain distinct individuals throughout the comparison.

Bringing life to the contours

Look again at the choices visible in the finished face: the color of the eyes, the warmth of the skin, the arrangement of the hair, and the direction of light.

Returning to the portrait with new sources

Later research brought the portrait into conversation with a wider body of evidence: the histories of the casts, journal entries about sittings, contemporary letters, later recollections, and printed source pages. Haven's letter and the BYU photograph enrich that later conversation without becoming earlier creation inputs.

A picture and the history behind it

The picture's history and Joseph's history meet here, but they answer different questions. The project record tells us how the likeness was used and which references it names. The historical sources help us understand individual features. Together they make the portrait more rewarding to study, while leaving room for further discovery.

Chapter 10

Discovery continues

This journey crosses journals, museum collections, scholarly studies, books, letters, and old periodicals. The Joseph Smith Papers, Church history collections, BYU publications, and digitized archives bring many of these scattered records within reach. Other material may still wait in an uncatalogued folder, a private collection, or an undigitized volume.

A letter brings the eyes into focus

One especially helpful encounter comes through Charlotte Haven. Her letter dated 22 January 1843, printed in 1890, describes blue eyes, a light complexion, youthful appearance, a stout build, and round shoulders. The printed page broadens our view beyond the features captured in a bust. Finding the autograph would help us understand the letter's path into print. 21

Art history offers discoveries of another kind. Davis's critique asks us to reconsider the weight given to the frontal oil. Hatch's account of the painting found in 1982 helps distinguish that object from a lost sitting drawing. Sometimes progress comes from identifying more precisely what a familiar image actually is. 22 · 23

When witnesses see differently

The witnesses also disagree. Caswall describes large hands, while the Gazette account describes small ones. Davis thought Joseph older than he was; other visitors remarked on youthfulness. Memory, feeling, editing, occasion, and viewpoint can all shape a description. Reading these differences together is more revealing than simply counting which words appear most often. 6 · 7 · 11 · 26

The next places to look

The original Gazette issue, Haven's autograph, and parts of the copying history of several portraits remain promising places to look. A closer study of the casts and carefully matched views could answer other questions. Each avenue would add a different kind of knowledge.

The sources do not close the subject. They invite us to keep looking with care: to value a small, well-grounded observation, to notice when accounts differ, and to let a new discovery deepen the human picture.

Chapter 11

What the evidence brings into focus

A recognizable pattern

Now bring the pictures back together. Across these different records, a broad pattern emerges: a long oval face, a prominent nose, a light complexion, and hair described in lighter tones. Our Joseph gathers these features into a mature, attentive face.

Look closely and the portrait becomes more particular. The cheeks carry warmth, the skin has fine texture, and the brows, lids, and mouth are subtly unequal. These small differences lend the face individuality. The historical sources support some broad choices far more strongly than they support individual lines or marks.

A face seen from more than one direction

The casts and portraits offer useful points of comparison, but their viewpoints, condition, and histories differ. Comparing them carefully helps us understand the shape of the face without mistaking an appealing visual resemblance for an exact measurement.

The unfinished question of hair

Hair remains one of the most interesting open questions. The portrait's swept waves give it a fuller arrangement than some descriptions suggest. Strand texture, styling, and the effects of light help explain that difference. 12

Room for a fuller understanding

The blue-gray eyes sit within a record that includes both blue and hazel descriptions. The complexion rests on broad descriptions of light skin. The surviving evidence does not yield an exact eye shade, a map of freckles, or a single expression that Joseph wore throughout his life.

What emerges is a portrait to consider alongside the sources, with appreciation for what they preserve and patience for what they leave unresolved. A newly found letter, a better understood art object, or a careful comparison could still deepen the picture.

Chapter 12

Questions worth carrying forward

What another look might reveal

As we finish, what would we still like to know? A useful next question is a specific one: which feature might a new source help us understand more clearly? A dated scene, a particular cast, and a clearly identified painting provide a firmer starting point than a general search for a perfect likeness.

A comparison that teaches us something

Joseph's appearance changed across his life. An image of him at thirty-six belongs to a different moment from one at thirty-eight. For either, the history of the chosen cast and artwork should travel with the image, including repairs, copies, and uncertainty about attribution.

A comparison becomes more informative when the views are similar and the surviving contours are clear. Perspective can make a forehead appear steeper or a nose shorter. Stretching one image to fit another may create a resemblance that was not there before. Facial-comparison guidance helps explain these problems; it does not turn this portrait study into an identification examination. 15

Hair offers a useful example of a focused question. A straighter arrangement could be considered beside the present waves while the other features stay the same. Lashes could be considered separately. These are possibilities for a future comparison, not stages already completed in the making of this portrait.

New discoveries can change the picture

A newly authenticated contemporary likeness, an original letter, or a repeatable comparison attentive to viewpoint and condition could change our understanding. The most valuable discovery may be a modest one that brings a single feature into clearer focus.

Keep the sources in conversation

Historical portraits reward continued looking. Let the object, the observer's words, and the date of the encounter inform one another. Where they differ, the difference itself may tell us something about Joseph's changing appearance or about the people who remembered him.
