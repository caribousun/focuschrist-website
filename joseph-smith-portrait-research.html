01 · The research

Our focusChrist Joseph

A portrait informed by history

What did Joseph Smith look like? No single surviving source answers the whole question. A cast preserves contours; a portrait records an artist’s choices; a description captures what one observer noticed. Here we follow those records to see what supports our portrait, what remains uncertain, and what could justify a change.

Why we kept this likeness

The sources we compared give us no clear reason to change the portrait’s facial structure. They leave room to explore a straighter hairstyle, while several details of Joseph’s appearance remain uncertain.

Separate source views retain their own proportions. They are not aligned, warped, or matched to an anatomical scale. Keep pose, lighting and the cast’s condition in mind.

03 · The research

The research verdict

Keep him as he stands

The evidence supports retaining the approved portrait. Exact anatomy or recognition by a contemporary is not established.

This conclusion follows an audit of the actual approved pixels, the museum mask histories, journal records, written descriptions and the supplied research. The portrait has a long oval face, prominent nose, lighter brown hair and fair complexion. It also contains pores, color variation, folds and unequal contours. Generic criticism that it is textureless or perfectly symmetric does not describe this image.

Why a rebuild is not presently warranted

No appropriately matched, reproducible comparison has shown a particular structural error in this portrait. Absence of a measured match is not evidence of a mismatch. A replacement should correct a demonstrated material inconsistency or implement an informed artistic choice, not manufacture certainty by starting over.

The most reasonable optional study

The swept waves and hair volume are less firmly constrained than broad facial structure. A restrained, straighter-haired variation could be useful, with light lashes considered separately. Natural texture and arranged hair are not identical questions. Neither change is currently a proven correction. [12]

What the conclusion does not claim

It does not assign an accuracy percentage, authenticate a photograph, recover an exact eye shade or identify every individual skin mark. It does not prescribe changes to the nose, jaw, eye spacing, iris color, age or scars. The current approved image remains the comparison baseline.

The independent research reviews converge on retaining the portrait. They differ on how strongly a hair change is warranted. That remaining artistic judgment is preserved rather than turned into unanimous certainty.

04 · The research

Masks and life-derived art

The mask as a structural anchor

The original molds date to 29 June 1844; the surviving casts have uncertain manufacture dates. They record postmortem surface contours. Copying, repairs, missing regions and soft-tissue changes limit literal comparison. Related casts are not independent anatomical witnesses. [1]

The mask comparison in the portrait study now uses this same newly consulted BYU photograph. It supplies source context, not a pose-matched overlay or proof of agreement, and is not established as an original creation input. [1, 20]

Maudsley: a documented sitting, several objects

The journal records a profile sitting on 25 June 1842. The museum says the original sitting drawing is unidentified. Hatch describes the 1982 discovery more precisely as a probable map-source painting; this may resolve Sweat's broader wording if different objects are meant. Neither should simply be equated with the sitting drawing. A profile constrains silhouette, not all frontal dimensions. [2, 3, 5, 23]

Rogers: use within its documented limits

September 1842 journal entries document painting sessions. The familiar oil is attributed to Rogers, with object history and artistic accuracy still requiring care. Curator Robert O. Davis challenges Hatch's scaling and camera-angle assumptions and argues for greater weight to the oil. Its criticism supplies no automatic correction to our portrait. [4, 5, 22]

The historical image and newly made artwork have different roles. Labels identify the surviving source and the interpreted scenes; neither is an anatomically registered comparison.

06 · The research

Trait audit: facial structure

Look closely · Follow the evidence

01 Face length and breadth

In our portrait. Long oval face, broad upper face narrowing toward the chin.

The historical evidence. Masks and the Gazette transcription constrain broad shape; no pose-controlled ratios for this image were established. [1, 11]

Our assessment. Retain; no justified narrowing or widening.

The portrait has a long oval outline, broad above and narrower toward the chin. Joseph’s surviving cast offers a useful contour reference; the later transmission of the Gazette description also calls his head an oblong oval. [1, 11]

This explains a resemblance visible today. It does not recover the original artist’s individual decisions.

What this can and cannot tell us

Different views, postmortem changes and cast condition prevent a measured face-width match.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

02 Forehead and brow structure

In our portrait. Broad textured forehead, fine lines and substantial brow.

The historical evidence. Masks and profiles are relevant; later Quincy wording is not a contemporary measurement. [1, 3, 8]

Our assessment. A proposed slope change first needs a matched profile.

The adopted forehead is broad, with restrained lines and a substantial brow. The cast preserves surface contour, while Quincy’s later memoir describes a retreating forehead. These are different kinds of evidence. [1, 8]

What this can and cannot tell us

A forehead’s apparent slope depends on viewpoint. Neither the cast photograph nor a verbal description fixes living wrinkles or hairline.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

03 Nose

In our portrait. Long prominent bridge, rounded tip and visible alar breadth.

The historical evidence. Masks and Maudsley matter most. A criticism of Rogers is not a demonstrated defect here. [1, 3, 5]

Our assessment. Retain; no evidence-derived rotation, narrowing or millimeter adjustment.

The portrait retains a prominent bridge and rounded nasal tip. The cast and the profile engraving provide different views of this central feature. [1, 3]

What this can and cannot tell us

The engraving passes through an artist and engraver; the cast photograph has its own angle and lighting. No exact profile registration was performed.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

04 Mouth and upper-lip region

In our portrait. Noticeable skin distance above the mouth, moderate upper lip and fuller lower lip.

The historical evidence. Skin distance and red-lip thickness are different observations. The transmitted Gazette description is not a precise anatomical measurement. [11]

Our assessment. Retain; define the feature before proposing a change.

The skin between nose and mouth is distinct from the red part of the upper lip. The portrait shows a moderate upper lip and fuller lower lip; the later Gazette transcription describes thin lips. These statements should not be treated as one measurement. [11]

What this can and cannot tell us

A closed postmortem mouth cannot determine every living mouth position or the appearance of a smile.

07 · The research

Trait audit: chin and living eyes

Look closely · Follow the evidence

05 Chin and jaw

In our portrait. Firm chin with rounded-square corners.

The historical evidence. Mask coverage, repairs and orientation limit comparison. Round/square verbal labels are not measurements. [1]

Our assessment. No justified width or projection correction has been demonstrated.

The adopted chin has rounded-square corners and a firm lower contour. The cast offers a physical reference for discussing that shape. [1]

What this can and cannot tell us

Cast damage, coverage and orientation limit chin-width and projection comparisons. No corrective measurement is established.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

06 Iris color

In our portrait. Blue-gray appearance in this rendering.

The historical evidence. Haven's 1843-dated letter, published in 1890, adds independent blue-eye support. Later blue recollections coexist with the Gazette hazel description, transmitted later. [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 21]

Our assessment. Blue is defensible, but exact shade is unresolved; no compulsory recoloring.

The adopted eyes are blue-gray. Haven’s 1843-dated letter, printed in 1890, describes blue eyes; the later Gazette transcription describes light hazel. [11, 21]

Blue-gray remains an artistic interpretation within conflicting descriptions. It was not recovered from plaster.

What this can and cannot tell us

Neither a death cast nor a monochrome portrait establishes pigmentation. Haven’s autograph and the original Gazette issue were not inspected.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

07 Eye depth and lids

In our portrait. Open attentive eyes, natural lower-lid folds and unequal contours.

The historical evidence. Closed postmortem lids do not determine living aperture; illumination affects apparent depth. [1]

Our assessment. Do not invent droop or copy the death-state eyelids as a living face.

The portrait gives Joseph open, attentive eyes with natural lid folds. The cast records closed lids; Haven’s printed description gives an impression of eyes set back in the head. [1, 21]

What this can and cannot tell us

Closed lids cannot fix living eye opening. Light and head angle also change the apparent depth of the eyes.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

08 Brows and lashes

In our portrait. Substantial brows; lashes less conspicuous than the Gazette description.

The historical evidence. Long light lashes are a source lead, not a measured specification. The original newspaper image remains unverified here. [11]

Our assessment. An understated lash study is optional, not a proven correction.

The portrait has substantial brows and relatively unobtrusive lashes. The Gazette description transmitted in 1916 emphasizes long, light lashes and light, thick brows. [11]

What this can and cannot tell us

This is one transmitted observer’s description, not a count or measurement of hairs. The cast does not resolve their color or living appearance.

08 · The research

Trait audit: hair and complexion

Look closely · Follow the evidence

09 Hair color

In our portrait. Brown with lighter strands and highlights.

The historical evidence. Descriptions support a lighter brown/dark-blond range, influenced in appearance by age and light. No exact colorimetry was established. [9, 11, 12]

Our assessment. Retain the general range; no precise color value is recoverable.

The artwork uses brown hair with lighter strands. Pratt’s retrospective account describes light hair, and Haven’s published letter also uses a broad light-hair description. [9, 21]

What this can and cannot tell us

These words do not determine an exact brown or blond shade. Age, illumination and terminology can affect the descriptions.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

10 Hair texture and arrangement

In our portrait. Swept hair with visible wave and volume.

The historical evidence. Fine, straight natural texture creates tension with the coiffed appearance; arranged waves alone do not establish natural curl. [12]

Our assessment. Less wave and volume is the strongest optional study, not a compelled identity correction.

The adopted hair is swept back with visible wave and volume. A description of fine hair concerns strand quality; a hairstyle concerns how the hair is arranged. The two should be considered separately. [11]

What this can and cannot tell us

The cast provides no scalp hairstyle, and an engraved coiffure cannot prove natural curl or a single everyday arrangement.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

11 Complexion and skin detail

In our portrait. Fair skin with warm/red cheeks, pores, freckle-like marks and folds.

The historical evidence. A light complexion has support; each mark and degree of redness remain interpretation. [9, 10, 11, 12]

Our assessment. The actual pixels defeat a blanket smoothing criticism. Do not add artificial grime or aging.

The portrait uses fair, warm skin with visible pores and reddish cheeks. Historical light-complexion descriptions offer broad support for a light palette, but not for each mark or degree of warmth. [9, 21]

What this can and cannot tell us

Plaster supplies no living skin color. Individual freckles, pores, redness and fine lines remain artistic choices.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

12 Facial hair

In our portrait. Clean-shaven appearance.

The historical evidence. Retrospective sparse-beard descriptions and Quincy's contemporary several-day growth can coexist with changing grooming. [8, 9]

Our assessment. Retain for a general portrait; stubble belongs to an appropriate dated scene.

The adopted portrait is clean-shaven. Pratt remembered very little beard, while Quincy described several days of growth during his May 1844 visit. Grooming can change between occasions. [8, 9]

What this can and cannot tell us

Quincy’s 1883 memoir is shown here as a later source; his contemporary letter is separate. Neither requires a permanent bearded identity.

09 · The research

Trait audit: age and presentation

Look closely · Follow the evidence

13 Apparent age

In our portrait. Mature adult, plausibly in his late thirties.

The historical evidence. Contemporary impressions disagree: Davis estimated older, Taggart younger-looking. Joseph died at thirty-eight. [6, 7]

Our assessment. Do not age him merely to look historical. Each future scene needs its actual date and age.

Joseph was 38 when he died in June 1844. Observers’ estimates of his apparent age differed: Davis thought him older in 1840, while Haven’s published letter called him youthful. [7, 21]

What this can and cannot tell us

An observer’s age impression is not his birth date. The portrait’s late-thirties appearance remains an interpretation.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

14 Expression

In our portrait. Calm, attentive gaze and closed mouth.

The historical evidence. Descriptions of manner do not prescribe one permanent expression. [6, 9]

Our assessment. Retain as an artistic choice. Historical recognition cannot be assigned a defensible percentage here.

The portrait’s calm attention is one chosen expression. Pratt’s memoir remembers an affable manner and an unforced smile; those impressions suggest movement and variety rather than one permanent look. [9]

What this can and cannot tell us

A fixed cast cannot recover personality or a living smile.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

15 Asymmetry and scars

In our portrait. Unequal brow, lid and mouth contours; no prominent diagnostic scar.

The historical evidence. An unproven photograph's supposed scar or eye droop does not establish Joseph's living marks.

Our assessment. Preserve natural variation; invent no diagnostic mark to manufacture agreement.

The adopted eyes, brows and mouth are not perfectly symmetrical. Retaining these visible differences avoids turning a living face into an idealized mirror image.

What this can and cannot tell us

No reliable evidence reviewed here establishes a prominent diagnostic facial scar. Uneven cast surfaces cannot simply be transferred into living scars.

Look closely · Follow the evidence

16 Build and clothing

In our portrait. Bust in a dark coat and white neckcloth.

The historical evidence. A bust cannot establish stature, weight or full-body shape. Quincy describes different occasion-specific clothing. [8, 9]

Our assessment. Retain the period-plausible presentation without certifying exact clothing or body fidelity.

The bust shows a dark coat and white neckcloth. Descriptions of Joseph’s stature and strength concern his whole body; they cannot be verified from this crop. Clothing descriptions also belong to particular occasions. [8, 9]

What this can and cannot tell us

This portrait does not establish height, weight or body proportions. Quincy’s later work clothes do not prescribe the clothes for every Joseph scene.

10 · The research

Decision and conditional construction

Present decision: retain

No replacement identity is proposed for approval now. Preserve this portrait.

A bounded study, if later chosen

First define a dated scene or an explicitly late-Nauvoo portrait at about age 36-38. Identify the exact mask objects, Maudsley variants and Rogers-attributed work, including copying and repair relationships. Disputed photographs are not ground truth.

Before any structural change, demonstrate a stable material discrepancy in appropriately matched views. Record missing contours, perspective limits and independent reviewer disagreement. Design landmarks can document source consistency; they cannot supply identity probabilities or a forensic authentication. Unrestricted warping can manufacture an apparent fit. [15]

If a visual study is selected, test restrained hair first, with lashes separately. Keep other traits fixed so the comparison is meaningful. Current evidence supplies no replacement nose, jaw, eye spacing, iris shade or scar.

Evidence that would change the verdict

A repeatable mismatch that survives source condition, pose and analyst uncertainty; a newly authenticated contemporary depiction; or new original documentation could change a trait. A pleasing overlay or subjective confidence cannot substitute for those results.

Approval remains separate

Any future variation should receive independent historical and visual review before use. Adopting a new identity or publishing it remains a separate owner decision. A well-presented study or a technically sound image does not itself establish historical accuracy.

11 · The research

How our portrait was created

Recorded creation
The project identity record names Joseph’s masks as primary proportional references and Hyrum’s as comparative. The original prompt and intermediate fitting records have not been recovered.
Actually examined afterward
The retained portrait’s visible features, historical descriptions, cast histories and source publications were qualitatively reviewed. Newly consulted evidence is identified as later research.
Still proposed
Pose-controlled measurements, blinded comparisons and a restrained hair or lash variation have not been performed. Museum photogrammetry records casts; it does not validate this portrait.

A documented artistic process

The project record describes a reconstruction informed by museum death-mask references. It does not establish a measured recovery of every living feature. [27]

The historical-identity record identifies Joseph's death masks as the primary proportional reference and Hyrum's as comparative material. It records the exact image adopted by Wyatt on 14 September 2026 as the identity reference for future Joseph scenes. [27]

A mask cannot supply living eye color, skin color, hair, expression, clothes or light. Those parts of the finished portrait are artistic choices informed to differing degrees by historical description and period context. Joseph and Hyrum were distinct people; comparison does not make their faces interchangeable.

What the creation record does not establish

The reviewed project record does not include a contemporaneous landmark measurement log or a complete original generation prompt. We therefore cannot certify that every dimension was measured against a cast, or that every source in this study was used during creation.

Creation and later research are separate

This audit examines the retained image against a broader historical record. Sources newly recovered during the audit strengthen or qualify our assessment; they are not retroactively described as original design inputs. The exact approved pixels remain the comparison baseline.

Why the distinction matters

A transparent process explains why the picture was made as it was. Historical accuracy still depends on the evidence for each feature. A documented workflow and owner approval establish provenance and acceptance of the artwork, not authentication of Joseph's exact living appearance.

12 · The research

Discovery coverage and remaining gaps

The investigation combined the supplied research with a broad, documented public-source search: Joseph Smith Papers, Church museum and library material, BYU Studies and RSC, digitized books and periodicals, newspaper catalogs, and methodological literature. This is not an exhaustive search of every internet page, archive or private collection.

What the expanded search changed

Haven's 1843-dated letter was verified in its 1890 publication. It strengthens the blue-eye choice and adds light complexion, youthful stout build and round shoulders. The original manuscript remains unchecked. These details do not validate every feature of a bust. [21]

Davis's critique shows why a mask-to-portrait ratio is not automatically decisive. His preference for greater weight to the frontal oil is a substantive alternative, not proof that the oil is exact. Hatch's map-source account also refines the identity of the Maudsley object rediscovered in 1982. [22, 23]

Conflicting descriptions stay visible

The descriptions do not all agree. Caswall's large hands contrast with the Gazette's small hands; youthful and older-looking impressions coexist. Hostility, memory, editing, viewpoint and occasion can affect descriptions. We retain the conflict instead of counting quotations as votes. [6, 7, 11, 26]

Still unresolved

The original Gazette issue, Haven autograph, complete chains for some art objects, and a controlled measurement of our portrait remain gaps. Additional nondigitized and private records may matter. None of those gaps is proof that the portrait is wrong; neither is their absence a reason to call it exact.

The revised conclusion is therefore specific: the evidence supports retaining this interpretation, but does not substantiate every visible trait or prove it is the closest image that could ever be made.
