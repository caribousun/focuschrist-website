A family on the road in 1856

John Rowe Moyle and his wife joined the Church in England in 1851. Five years later, they emigrated with their children. Moyle was forty-eight when the family joined Edmund Ellsworth’s handcart company for the overland journey to Utah.

The company reached Salt Lake City on September 26, 1856. Their overland journey was the final stage of a migration that had begun across the Atlantic. Before following Moyle into his work in Utah, we can return to his own account of that earlier crossing.

Before the plains: across the Atlantic

Before the handcarts came an ocean voyage. Moyle’s own journal records leaving Liverpool aboard the Samuel Curling on April 19, 1856. He and his family were bound for a new home far from England. At sea, storms rolled their luggage about and left beds wet.

They reached Boston on May 23. Three days later they continued by rail, arriving in Iowa on June 2. After about a week in camp, they set out with Ellsworth’s first handcart company. The ocean crossing, the railway journey, and the preparations in Iowa brought them to the beginning of the long walk across the plains.

A skill offered to a shared undertaking

The Moyles settled in Alpine, south of Salt Lake City. Their home and farm were there, while the temple site lay about twenty-two miles away. Moyle was an accomplished stonecutter, and his skill was wanted for the temple. He undertook the journey on foot, leaving the family’s horse available for work at home.

His stone house survives at the historic Moyle property, now an Alpine city park. The home preserves the setting of the family life and farm work that continued alongside his temple service.

The distance between home and the work

The weekly journey began before daylight. In the account retold by Dieter F. Uchtdorf, Moyle left home at about two o’clock on Monday morning to reach work by eight. He stayed in Salt Lake City during the week. After work ended on Friday evening, he walked home again, arriving around midnight.

During those working weeks he lodged with a son in Salt Lake City. Friday’s return took him back to the farm and family in Alpine; Monday brought the long outward journey again.

Making a way to walk again

An injury eventually cost Moyle a leg. During his recovery, he made a wooden replacement with an articulated ankle and foot. He practiced walking on it, gradually rebuilding the endurance needed for the journey to Salt Lake City. Uchtdorf’s account describes pain and persistence as he learned to use it.

The Ensign Peak Foundation’s site history reports that Moyle’s wooden leg is displayed at the Daughters of Utah Pioneers Museum in Salt Lake City.

Remembering the worker beyond the obstacle

Moyle returned to temple work. Learning to walk with the wooden leg enabled him to take up the journeys and skilled labor that had filled his working weeks. The story remembered his persistence through the loss of a leg and his effort to keep serving.

Holiness to the Lord

The words associated with Moyle’s most remembered work are “Holiness to the Lord.” A later account supplied by his family describes him climbing scaffolding on the east side of the Salt Lake Temple to carve the inscription. His work gave lasting expression to the purpose of the building he had helped construct.

The inscription remains part of the temple. An official 2025 restoration report credits Moyle and describes renewed gilding of the panel on the east center tower. Moyle died on February 15, 1889, before the temple’s 1893 dedication. The words he carved outlasted him, still identifying the temple as a house of the Lord.
