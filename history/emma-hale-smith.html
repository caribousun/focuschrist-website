Emma Hale Smith: a life of faith, work, and care

Emma’s story reaches beyond any single moment in Church history.

Follow her from Pennsylvania to Nauvoo through a young family, songs of faith, organized relief and the work of carrying on after loss.

A life begins beside the Susquehanna

Emma Hale was born on July 10, 1804, to Isaac Hale and Elizabeth Lewis Hale, the seventh of their nine children. She grew up in Pennsylvania’s Susquehanna Valley, among the fields, woods and river of a close-knit family community. Her mother took her to Methodist worship, and stories of Emma’s childhood remember her seeking God in prayer.

Emma received an education and later taught school. The young woman who would one day help prepare a book of hymns and serve as a scribe had already learned to read, write and teach. Explore Emma’s Susquehanna for more about the place where she grew up.

In 1825 Joseph Smith boarded with the Hale family, and Emma met the young man who would become her husband. Her parents opposed the match. Emma and Joseph nevertheless chose to marry on January 18, 1827, in South Bainbridge, New York. She was twenty-two; he was twenty-one. Their shared life would take them far from the Susquehanna and into the beginnings of the Restoration.

The right person

In the first hours of September 22, 1827, Joseph Smith prepared to go to the hill where the gold plates were buried. The angel Moroni had told him to bring the right person with him, and Joseph knew through personal revelation that the right person was Emma. She waited while he received the plates and hid them in a hollow log, and she rode home beside him as the sun rose. From the very first night of the work, Emma was not watching from a distance. She was in the carriage.

She kept watch in the months that followed. When word came that men were plotting to steal the plates, Emma took a spare horse and rode more than an hour to warn Joseph, who moved the record to safety. On the journey to Pennsylvania she helped hide the plates in a barrel of beans. At Harmony she gave Joseph a linen cloth to cover the plates as they rested on the kitchen table, and she asked her brother-in-law for a box that stayed under their bed, where the plates were locked each night. Her care was practical and constant, the kind of faithfulness that shows up in cloth and boxes and borrowed horses.

A pen, a young family, a calling

In the spring of 1828, twenty-three-year-old Emma was expecting her first child while helping with the translation at Harmony.

A pen in her hand

Emma also served as Joseph's first scribe. She sat with him during the early translation of the Book of Mormon and wrote down the words as he dictated them, carefully setting down verses that families now read together. She sewed a pouch for a seer stone Joseph sometimes used in the translation. Long afterward she remembered that season plainly: "I was an active participant," she said. She did not describe herself as a bystander in her own story, and we should not either.

Those same months brought deep sorrow. On June 15, 1828, Emma gave birth to their first son. He died the same day, and Emma herself was gravely ill. Their young family’s story began with a loss that cannot be separated from the work taking place at Harmony. The Smith family history follows their children’s lives.

Emma’s place in the translation story

Emma took part in the early translation work. Martin Harris and Oliver Cowdery also served as scribes, with Oliver writing most of the manuscript that became the published Book of Mormon. Thou Art an Elect Lady places her work and the 1830 revelation in context.

Called by name

Emma was baptized on June 28, 1830, in Colesville, New York. A crowd gathered and her confirmation was delayed, and Joseph was arrested, but the following month, in July 1830, a revelation came that spoke to Emma directly. It called her "an elect lady, whom I have called." It asked her to be a comfort to Joseph "in his afflictions, with consoling words, in the spirit of meekness." It set her to expound the scriptures and exhort the Church, and it gave her a charge no one else received: to make a selection of sacred hymns for the Saints.

Notice the order of that calling. Comfort came first, before teaching, before the hymns. In the revelation's own framing, her ministry begins with consoling words spoken in meekness to one suffering person she loved. Watch for that pattern as her story goes on: a woman who knew how to sit with another person's pain and stay.

The family moved to Ohio in 1831. That spring Emma gave birth to twins who died soon after birth. Soon afterward Emma and Joseph adopted the newborn Murdock twins, Julia and Joseph, whose mother had died. The little boy died the following March while suffering from measles, after a mob attacked Joseph Smith. The family’s losses and its welcome to two children were intertwined. Joseph and Emma Hale Smith Family follows the children’s lives.

Emma was grieving her own children while caring for Joseph and Julia. Welcoming them did not erase those losses.

Songs and separation

A son, Joseph III, was born in November 1832.

A charge of her own

Doctrine and Covenants 25 promised Emma that "the song of the righteous is a prayer unto me," and that the Lord delights in "the song of the heart." The hymnbook was not a side task handed to her between other duties. It stood in the revelation beside comforting her husband and expounding the scriptures, one of the defining works of her life.

She began gathering early. Working with William W. Phelps, she selected hymns that were printed in Church newspapers in 1832, so Saints in different settlements could begin singing the same words. At a time when choosing hymns was work usually reserved for male clergy, a woman in her twenties decided what the Church would sing.

By the autumn of 1835, Emma was thirty-one and bringing the hymn collection toward publication.

A book of words

The first Latter-day Saint hymnbook was prepared at Kirtland: A Collection of Sacred Hymns for the Church of the Latter Day Saints. The edition is dated 1835, and its title page names the hymns as selected by Emma Smith. The printing ran late, though. The Joseph Smith Papers, which publishes the book, notes that although it bears an 1835 date it was apparently not available until early 1836. It held ninety hymn texts and no musical notation at all, just words, gathered so that families and branches who had no songbook and perhaps no instrument could still carry the tunes they knew and learn new ones. The little book traveled where the Saints traveled, into homes and small meeting rooms, and it treated singing as something the whole church did together, not something performed for them.

Open that collection today and you will meet hymns the Church still sings. Her book carried "How Firm a Foundation," with its promise to the Saint who feels outnumbered by trouble: “Fear not, I am with thee”. Generations who never learned Emma's name by heart have leaned on words she chose for them. That is a particular kind of leadership, quiet enough to be mistaken for background, and it is one of the most durable things she built.

Why her choices lasted

Emma selected for people who were suffering. The Saints of the 1830s were being gathered, scattered, and gathered again, and the hymns she favored speak of trust in God, the Savior's care, and courage to continue. Her collection taught doctrine the way Doctrine and Covenants 25 said it would: as prayer. A family that could not yet afford a hymnal of its own could still be taught, verse by verse, at the kitchen table.

Her own life kept testing the words. In the years after the book appeared she buried children, crossed a frozen river with four little ones, and organized relief for a city of refugees. Knowing her life, it is hard to read "the song of the heart" as only a fine phrase. It sounds like prayer that had to be carried, and was carried.

Frederick was born in 1836, and Alexander in 1838.

A family separated

The Saints had been driven from their homes in Missouri under threat of violence, and Joseph Smith and other Church leaders were held in Liberty Jail. Emma visited her husband there and carried letters between the prison and the scattered Saints. At home in Far West she had four children to feed and protect: Joseph III, who was six; Julia, seven; Frederick, two; and Alexander, a baby of eight months. The Saints were leaving Missouri for Illinois, and Emma had to get her family across the Mississippi River in the middle of winter.

The ice

In February 1839 Emma crossed the frozen Mississippi River on foot with her children, carrying eight-month-old Alexander. She also carried something entrusted to her care: the manuscript of Joseph's translation of the Bible, tucked beneath her skirt so it would cross safely with her. Think about what she chose to protect on that walk. Her children in her arms and against her skirt, and under her skirt, pages of scripture she believed the world would need. Faith, in Emma's life, was rarely an abstraction. It was weight she was willing to carry.

On the far shore lay Quincy, Illinois, where the residents opened their town to thousands of homeless Saints. Emma and the children were taken in, and the family found shelter and work while they waited for word about Joseph.

Her own words

Emma was thirty-four when she wrote from Quincy in March 1839.

From Quincy, on March 7, 1839, Emma wrote to Joseph in Liberty Jail. She told him about the children and about the kindness the family had received. She did not hide what the winter had cost her. She wrote of the suffering she had passed through since the militia came into Far West, and then she wrote the sentence her descendants have quoted ever since: "I still live and am yet willing to suffer more if it is the will of kind Heaven, that I should for your sake."

Read that sentence slowly. She does not claim the suffering was small. She does not promise she understands it. She says she is still here, and still willing. Emma's endurance was not a stiff upper lip. It was a decision, renewed in Quincy after she had left her home in Missouri, to keep loving and keep going for the people who depended on her.

Joseph escaped custody that spring and rejoined his family in Illinois, and the Saints began building again at the settlement that became Nauvoo. Emma's crossing was not the end of her hardships. But whenever the story of the Restoration tells of faith under pressure, her footprints are already on the ice.

Emma’s letter of March 7, 1839 lets us hear about the separation in her own words, from the work of caring for the children to her gratitude for the people who helped them. By May the family was in Commerce, the settlement that became Nauvoo.

Relief and difficult questions

Building a home in Nauvoo did not end the family’s losses. Don Carlos, born in 1840, died at fourteen months. Another son reportedly died on the day of his birth in February 1842. Her responsibilities as a mother continued alongside the service she would undertake among Nauvoo’s women.

Emma was thirty-seven at the March 1842 meeting, just weeks after the loss of her infant son.

A seamstress's idea, a prophet's pattern

The society began with practical kindness. Margaret Cook, a seamstress, noticed that the men building the Nauvoo Temple needed shirts, and she and her employer Sarah Kimball gathered friends to sew. When their draft constitution reached Joseph Smith, he said the Lord had something better in mind: the women would be organized after the pattern of the priesthood and given a lasting place in the Church.

On March 17, 1842, twenty women met in the large room above Joseph's Red Brick Store. They elected Emma president by unanimous vote, and she chose Sarah Cleveland and Elizabeth Ann Whitney as her counselors. Joseph read to them the 1830 revelation given to Emma, the one that called her an elect lady, and taught that she was to expound the scriptures and teach the women of the community, and that other women could attain the same blessings. Then Emma rose and set the tone for everything that followed: “We are going to do something extraordinary. ... We expect extraordinary occasions and pressing calls.”

Reading the first meeting

The first meeting brought together plans for the new society and practical questions about helping neighbors. The women discussed those in distress, the needs of widows and fair payment for their work. Relief Society founding minutes opens the record of that conversation.

Relief that relieved

Joseph taught the society two purposes: it was "not only to relieve the poor, but to save souls." Under Emma's presidency the women did both with their sleeves rolled up. They found families in distress, carried food and clothing to destitute immigrants arriving from Missouri and the British Isles, arranged exchanges of work so that widows could be paid fairly for their labor, and kept careful minutes of all of it. Emma insisted that care include honest dealing, not only kind feelings. At home she practiced the same pattern, opening her own door to the sick, the orphaned, and the homeless in a city where malaria moved from house to house.

In July 1842 Emma joined Eliza R. Snow and Amanda Barnes Smith in carrying a Relief Society petition to Illinois governor Thomas Carlin in Quincy. They asked the governor to protect Joseph from extradition and their community from threats of violence. Their journey brought the society’s concern for its neighbors into public view. Read about the petition.

The society grew quickly. By March 1844, more than 1,300 women had been admitted as members. Emma taught doctrine in the meetings, guided who should be received into the society, and spoke plainly about moral purity. The women counseled one another, bore testimony, and discussed the scriptures together. Lucy Mack Smith, who told the sisters she feared she could not meet with them many more times because of her age, stood among them and left her testimony that the Book of Mormon is the book of God.

A pattern that outlasted Nauvoo

The Nauvoo society met through 1842 and 1843. In March 1844 Emma spoke at four meetings on the need for moral purity, and after those meetings the society did not gather again in Nauvoo. It might have become a closed chapter. Instead, Eliza R. Snow carried the society's minute book west, and when Brigham Young later asked her to help reestablish the Relief Society in Utah, she used that book as the pattern. Ward by ward, women organized again with Nauvoo's minutes open in front of them, recounting the founding story and Emma's presidency as their beginning.

That is why March 17, 1842 still matters to a sister who joins Relief Society today. The society she enters was shaped at its start by a woman who expected extraordinary occasions, and who believed that relieving the poor and saving souls belonged in the same pair of hands.

Emma’s marriage also faced serious difficulty over Joseph’s practice of plural marriage. She temporarily approved some marriages, likely did not know about all the sealings, and at other times opposed the practice; by 1844 she opposed it. The Church’s Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo discusses this history. The conflict was a painful part of their marriage. Her changing responses deserve careful attention alongside her years of family life and service.

On June 27, 1844, Joseph and his brother Hyrum were killed at Carthage. Emma was thirty-nine and pregnant. Her responsibilities now included mourning her husband and preparing to raise the child who would be born after his death.

A household to sustain

A widow's work

After Joseph’s death, Emma had children to support and urgent questions to answer. On July 17, 1844, the Hancock County probate court appointed her to administer Joseph’s estate and serve as guardian of her four children. The Joseph Smith Papers follows the estate proceedings.

In November 1844, nearly five months after Joseph’s death, Emma gave birth to their son David Hyrum Smith. Joseph had left no legal will, and disagreements grew between Emma and Brigham Young over property held in Joseph's name and the debts attached to it, questions that mattered to the Church and to a widow counting what she could keep for her children. She held on to the Mansion House and what she could claim for her family's support.

When most of the Saints left Nauvoo for the west in 1846, Emma stayed. During the violence of that year she took temporary refuge in Fulton, Illinois, and returned to Nauvoo a few months later, early in 1847. Illinois remained her home for the rest of her life. Staying was not the absence of a decision. It was her decision, made for her children and herself, and she lived with its costs and its dignities alike.

A second marriage and a full house

On December 23, 1847, Emma married Lewis C. Bidamon, a Nauvoo resident who was not a member of the Church. Their household continued in Nauvoo through the following decades. Emma’s story did not end with widowhood; she made a life for herself and her family through the years that followed.

A different path, told plainly

In 1860 Emma affiliated with the Reorganized Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, today Community of Christ, in the same year her son Joseph III became that church’s president. Her path after 1844 was not the path of the Saints who went west, and members of both churches have sometimes told her story as if it must belong to only one of them. She remained in Nauvoo, raised her family in the community she chose, and kept her faith in the things she had witnessed, even as her church affiliation differed from the one her husband had founded and Brigham Young led west.

Remembering these years keeps Emma from disappearing at the end of Joseph’s life. She lived for nearly thirty-five more years, with relationships, work and decisions of her own. Her story asks us to make room for those years too.

The remaining years

Frederick died in 1862. Of Emma’s nine biological children and two adopted children, five reached adulthood; Frederick’s death brought another loss after those early bereavements.

By the autumn of 1871, Emma was sixty-seven. She and Lewis had moved into the completed portion of the Nauvoo House, operated as the Riverside Mansion boardinghouse. Welcoming boarders and caring for the household became part of these later years, nearly three decades after Joseph’s death.

The Riverside Mansion was Emma’s home for the last eight years of her life. The Nauvoo House was a different building from the Mansion House, where she and Lewis had lived before. Nauvoo House and Riverside Mansion describes the setting; My Great-Great-Grandmother, Emma Hale Smith traces this part of her family’s chronology.

In 1877, David’s severe mental-health difficulties led to hospitalization. His illness brought lasting concern to the family, and he remained in institutional care for the rest of his life.

The testimony she kept

In the last months of her life, Emma spoke with her sons about her memories and her faith. In an interview with Joseph III, she spoke about the Book of Mormon she had helped bring into the world as a young scribe at Harmony. This is what she said: "My belief is that the Book of Mormon is of divine authenticity. I have not the slightest doubt of it."

She had held the plates' box under her bed. She had written at the translation table. She had carried a later manuscript under her skirt across a frozen river. At seventy-four, when her son asked her about that book, she told him she had not the slightest doubt of it. Emma died in Nauvoo on April 30, 1879, and she is buried beside Joseph.

In 1845, Lucy Mack Smith praised Emma’s “unflinching courage, zeal and patience.” That tribute speaks to the many years Emma still had ahead of her. She was not only the woman who stood beside the Prophet at the beginning. She was a witness in her own right, and she remained one.
