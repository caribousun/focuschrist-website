Priesthood authority and the call to serve

Doctrine and Covenants 27:12–13 names Peter, James, and John and connects their ministry with apostolic authority and keys. Doctrine and Covenants 128:20 remembers their voices in the wilderness between Harmony and Colesville, along the Susquehanna River.

Begin with those witnesses and the questions they actually answer. The opening picture shows a priesthood blessing today. The historical account below traces a different moment: the restoration of authority through Peter, James, and John. The surviving sources do not establish a precise date for the conferral.

Peter, James, and John: authority and unfolding understanding

Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery testified that Peter, James, and John conferred greater priesthood authority upon them. The Church's historical account explains that surviving sources do not establish the exact date. Estimates differ, and the restoration should not be assigned a precise day simply to complete a timeline. Many detailed accounts were written later; earlier references are less specific. Study the evidence and the dating question.

A ministry received by both men

The accounts do not describe the apostles’ arrangement or establish which man received this ministry first.

Explore the evidence: the place, the date, and unfolding understanding

Joseph's 1842 letter, now Doctrine and Covenants 128:20, places their voices in the wilderness between Harmony and Colesville, along the Susquehanna. Doctrine and Covenants 27:12–13 connects these messengers with apostolic authority and the keys of ministry. These passages preserve the central claim without providing a complete scene description.

Understanding also developed over time. The Church's account follows later revelations that clarified priesthood offices and terminology, and subsequent restoration of keys and temple ordinances. It presents the restoration as a sequence of events rather than a single moment containing every later organizational detail. Keeping these developments in view helps us read early records in their own setting.

Priesthood and temple blessings: lives, restrictions, and the 1978 revelation

This history follows Black Latter-day Saints whose faith and service continued while priesthood and temple restrictions limited their participation. Their lives help us understand both what was withheld and what changed in 1978.

Elijah Able: ordained, serving, and still asking

Elijah Able, whose name also appears as Abel, joined the Church in 1832. In Kirtland in 1836, Ambrose Palmer ordained him to the Melchizedek Priesthood. He participated in washing and anointing in the Kirtland Temple, and Zebedee Coltrin ordained him a Seventy. His service took him to New York and Canada as a missionary. Later he worked as a carpenter, raised a family with Mary Ann Adams, and helped build the Salt Lake Temple.

His life also exposes the distinction between holding priesthood and receiving later temple ordinances. Although he retained his priesthood standing after the racial restriction was announced, his request to receive his endowment and be sealed to Mary Ann was denied. Another request was refused in 1879. The earlier Kirtland ceremonies did not mean that he had received the later endowment. He continued in the Church until his death in 1884. His service and his exclusion belong in the same account.

The restriction and the people it excluded

In 1852, Brigham Young publicly announced that men of Black African descent could no longer be ordained. Black people could still join the Church through baptism and confirmation. Over the following decades, restrictions also excluded Black women and men from temple endowment and sealing. These were profound limits on participation, including the opportunity to covenant in the temple and be sealed as families. They did not amount to a prohibition on every ordinance: some Black Saints performed baptisms for deceased relatives.

Jane Manning James: faith with an unanswered petition

Jane’s own remembered life reaches beyond the petitions preserved in institutional records. In the autobiography she dictated near the end of her life, she wanted her faith and experience to be heard at her funeral. Her life included family responsibilities, help given to neighbors, and devotion sustained through hardship. She remembered her belief in Jesus Christ’s gospel as stronger in old age than when she first joined the Church.

Between 1884 and 1904, Jane repeatedly approached Church leaders about receiving her endowment and being sealed. In 1888 she received permission to perform baptisms for deceased relatives. In 1894, leaders arranged a unique proxy sealing that placed her in Joseph Smith’s household as a servant. That arrangement did not grant the endowment and family sealings she had sought. Jane died in 1908 without receiving them during her lifetime. Her endowment and family sealings were performed on her behalf in 1979. Remembering her faithfully requires hearing both her testimony and her persistent request for blessings withheld from her.

Faith that crossed borders

By the 1970s, the question touched believers across several continents. Black and mixed-ancestry members in Brazil contributed toward the São Paulo Temple while knowing that the restriction would keep some of them from its ordinances. In Ghana and Nigeria, groups studied the restored gospel and asked the Church to send missionaries. These were people seeking baptism, service, and a place in a worshipping community—not merely an administrative question about ancestry.

June 1978: prayer, confirmation, and an open way

On June 1, 1978, President Kimball met with members of the First Presidency and Quorum of the Twelve in the Salt Lake Temple. Gordon B. Hinckley, who was present, later recalled discussion followed by a prayer voiced by Kimball. His account describes a shared assurance through the Holy Ghost that the time had come to extend priesthood blessings without the racial restriction. This is the experience the participants testified of: a sacred spiritual confirmation received as they prayed together.

Receiving the blessings and rejecting racism

The change reached women as well as men through access to temple ordinances and family sealing. It did not create priesthood ordination for women. For families who had waited, the opened way meant concrete opportunities to serve, make covenants, and receive ordinances together. Jane’s ordinances in 1979 also connect the new access with the life of someone who had asked long before the revelation.

Today the Church rejects teachings that connect Black skin with divine disfavor or supposed failure before birth, oppose marriage across races, or rank one race above another. Its current teaching calls members to repent of prejudice and help remove racism. These commitments belong beside the history, not outside it. Learning these lives can move us toward more careful listening, truthful remembrance, and a willingness to welcome one another in the community of Jesus Christ.

Authority and a life of service

Doctrine and Covenants 121:34–46 places the exercise of priesthood within a demanding moral teaching. Persuasion, long-suffering, gentleness, meekness, love unfeigned, kindness, and pure knowledge are part of the passage’s language. Read its warnings as well as its promises.

These verses do not excuse pressure or mistreatment in the name of authority. They invite examination of how people are actually treated. In daily relationships, listening patiently and serving without seeking control are useful places to begin reflecting on the passage.
