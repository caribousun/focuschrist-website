How Do Prayer and Personal Revelation Work?

Answers / Prayer and revelation

Latter-day Saints believe Heavenly Father hears sincere prayer and can give personal guidance through the Holy Ghost. They also believe spiritual impressions should be sought humbly, tested carefully, and kept within the responsibilities God has actually given a person.

Personal revelation is a religious belief and spiritual practice, not a substitute for professional, legal, medical, or emergency help when those are needed.

Prayer Is Communication with Heavenly Father

Latter-day Saint prayer is directed to God the Father in the name of Jesus Christ. Prayer can include gratitude, confession, worship, requests for help, questions, and quiet listening. The purpose is not to force a preferred answer from God but to turn toward Him with sincerity and willingness to follow Him.

What Is Personal Revelation?

Personal revelation means divine guidance given to an individual for that person’s life, needs, relationships, and responsibilities. Latter-day Saints believe the Holy Ghost can bring peace, warning, understanding, remembrance, conviction, or quiet impressions. Revelation may come gradually rather than as a dramatic event.

How Can Someone Prepare to Receive Guidance?

Official Church teaching emphasizes prayer, scripture study, repentance, obedience, worship, and patient effort. A person may study a question, make a thoughtful decision, ask God for confirmation or correction, and remain open to an answer that differs from the original preference.

How Can Spiritual Impressions Be Discerned?

Latter-day Saints are taught not to treat every feeling as revelation. Genuine guidance should be consistent with God’s revealed commandments and the teachings of Jesus Christ. It should not authorize a person to control someone else’s agency or claim revelation for responsibilities that belong to another. Time, scripture, wise counsel, repeated impressions, and the fruits produced by a decision can help with discernment.

What If No Answer Comes?

Waiting for an answer can be lonely, especially when the question matters deeply. A quiet prayer or an uncertain feeling is not, by itself, a verdict on your faith. Latter-day Saint teaching makes room for guidance that comes gradually and for thoughtful decisions made without a detailed instruction. You can keep seeking, talk with someone you trust, and take time to gather what you need.

A Simple Place to Start

Read James 1:5-6, which invites those seeking wisdom to ask God. Doctrine and Covenants 8:2-3 describes guidance through the Holy Ghost in the mind and heart. You might name one question in your own words, spend a little time with the passage, and write down what you still need to understand. This is an invitation to pray, not a test of whether you feel something immediately.

When You Are Unsure Whether a Feeling Is Guidance

You can take an impression seriously while still considering it carefully. What does scripture teach about the decision? What information is missing? Does the choice respect another person's freedom? An urgent feeling alone does not settle those questions.

In A Framework for Personal Revelation, Dale G. Renlund discusses scripture, personal responsibility, God's commandments, and openness to further guidance. His explanation is especially helpful when someone claims to know God's will for another person's major life decisions. Counsel can help you think; it does not remove your responsibility to choose.

If your concern is that answers seem slow, the Church's Recognize That Revelation Is a Process offers further study. You might return to one question over time, without treating each prayer as a deadline for certainty.

Three Passages for a More Thoughtful Prayer Life

Prayer without performance

Matthew 6:5-13 places Jesus's teaching on prayer beside cautions about public display and empty repetition. The prayer He teaches includes God's will, daily needs, forgiveness, and deliverance. Consider which part is closest to your concern. You may pray in ordinary words; a polished explanation is not a prerequisite for seeking God.

Study and responsibility

Doctrine and Covenants 9:7-9 was addressed to Oliver Cowdery in connection with translation. It describes studying a matter and asking God. Keep that setting in view: the passage should not become a rule that every faithful person must experience the same physical sensation for every decision. Compare it with the broader discernment guidance linked above.

Faith can begin before certainty

Alma 32:21, 26-28 distinguishes faith from perfect knowledge and invites a willingness to begin. As a personal exercise, separate what you know, what you hope, and what you still need to learn. That can help you bring a more honest question to prayer without claiming certainty you do not have.

What should I write down?

Consider the question, information you have gathered, counsel you have received, and a possible next step within your responsibility. Revisit it as circumstances change. A journal can support reflection, but it does not turn every thought into revelation.
