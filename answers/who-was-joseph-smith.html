Joseph Smith (1805-1844) was the founding prophet of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Latter-day Saints believe God called him to help restore the gospel and Church of Jesus Christ in the latter days.

Stand Forever · Third foundational question

Was Joseph Smith a prophet?

Joseph Smith reported visions, brought forth the Book of Mormon, organized the Church in 1830, and taught doctrines that continue to shape Latter-day Saint faith. His prophetic claim can be examined through his firsthand accounts, contemporary records, the texts he presented as scripture, and the lasting fruits of his ministry.

  1. Begin with the accounts. Read the First Vision sources linked below. Notice who recorded each account, when it was recorded, and which question you are asking of it.
  2. Study a scriptural claim. Follow the Book of Mormon references and the Harmony study. Distinguish what a source claims from what a later interpretation adds.
  3. Continue the inquiry. Use the reviewed videos and reflection questions to identify a useful next source. Then explore the restored Church study for the beliefs, ordinances, and community connected with this history.

Joseph Smith: The Prophet of the Restoration

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints

The Church's First Vision Accounts essay identifies four firsthand accounts. Its discussion of the 1832 account emphasizes Joseph's concern for forgiveness and personal redemption. The 1838 account places the vision within the beginnings of the Church and emphasizes his question about which church to join.

These are different dates of recording, not four dates for the event. Begin with one account, noting its audience and emphasis. Then compare a second. The essay presents the Church's explanation of differences and links to documents so that you can examine more than a modern summary.

The First Vision

Joseph Smith grew up during intense religious revival in the northeastern United States. As a teenager, he sought divine guidance about which church to join. He later recorded that in answer to prayer he saw God the Father and Jesus Christ. Latter-day Saints call this experience the First Vision and regard it as the beginning of the Restoration.

The Book of Mormon

Joseph said an angel named Moroni later directed him to an ancient record written on metal plates. He translated the record by the gift and power of God, and it was published in 1830 as the Book of Mormon. The book presents itself as another testament of Jesus Christ.

The Restoration of the Church

Latter-day Saints believe priesthood authority, gospel ordinances, prophetic revelation, and the organization of Christ’s Church were restored through Joseph Smith. The Church was formally organized in 1830. Joseph served as its first president and received revelations that now form a substantial portion of the Doctrine and Covenants.

Joseph Smith Was Not the Object of Worship

Latter-day Saints honor Joseph as a prophet, but worship God the Father in the name of Jesus Christ. Joseph’s prophetic role is meaningful to believers because they understand his mission as pointing people to Jesus Christ, restoring priesthood authority, and bringing forth scripture that testifies of Christ.

A Historical Figure Worth Studying Carefully

Joseph Smith’s life included religious leadership, migration, conflict, legal controversy, political activity, plural marriage, and violence directed both toward and around the early Saints. A responsible study should not reduce him to either a flawless hero or a caricature. The Church’s history resources include primary-source context and essays for difficult questions as well as devotional accounts.

If a Historical Question Troubles You

You can examine one question at a time. Begin with the linked accounts, notice when and why each was written, and distinguish what the record says from later interpretation. If you are discussing the question with someone else, make room for what it means to them as well as the evidence. You do not need to rush past a concern to keep learning.

Follow a Historical Question to the Records

The Church's First Vision Accounts essay discusses differences among the accounts and explains the Church's interpretation of them. Its notes provide a path to the underlying records. Read an account before its commentary when you can, and distinguish Joseph's reported experience from a historian's explanation of the record.

A devotional account and a historical essay may serve different purposes. Asking when a text was written, who preserved it, and what evidence is available is a reasonable part of study. You can hold a specific question open while examining it carefully.

A Worked Example: Studying the First Vision

Read the account before the summary

Joseph Smith History 1:5-20 recounts religious disagreement, Joseph's reading and prayer, and his reported vision. Record the questions he describes and the response he says he received. This is the scriptural account accepted by Latter-day Saints; identifying it as an account is important when studying history.

Compare accounts with attention to when they were recorded

Use the First Vision Accounts essay linked above to reach the other records. Compare emphasis, wording, audience, and date of recording. The date an event is said to have occurred and the date a surviving account was written are different facts. The essay offers the Church's interpretation of differences; the underlying documents allow you to examine the evidence yourself.

Keep historical and spiritual questions distinct

A historical investigation can ask what records survive and how they relate. A spiritual inquiry may ask what the Restoration claim means for belief and practice. Those inquiries can inform each other, but one should not silently stand in for the other. If a concern remains, name it specifically and pursue the relevant evidence.

A useful source note

Write the title, author or recorder, date of the record, and exact passage you are considering. Add a separate sentence for your interpretation. This keeps a later retelling from becoming indistinguishable from the original evidence.

A family remembered by Lucy Mack Smith

1812–1813

Praying beside their daughter

A fever swept through the Smith household in Lebanon, New Hampshire. As Sophronia grew dangerously weak, Lucy and Joseph Sr. knelt beside her bed and prayed. Lucy remembered gathering her daughter into her arms and watching anxiously as her breathing returned.

Their faith took the form of staying close. Before we meet Joseph as a preacher or translator, we meet a family learning to carry fear together and to turn toward God while caring for one another.

1813

A brother keeps watch

During Joseph’s long illness, Hyrum offered to take his exhausted mother’s place beside the bed. Lucy remembered him holding Joseph’s painful leg for hours, trying to ease his younger brother’s suffering.

Joseph was about seven; Hyrum was twelve or thirteen. The devotion we later see between these brothers began in the ordinary courage of a child who would not leave his brother alone.

1816

A mother’s faith travels with her daughter

Before Lucy followed her husband to Palmyra, she said farewell to her mother, Lydia Gates Mack. Lydia encouraged her daughter to remain faithful and spoke of the hope that they would meet again beyond this life.

The move carried more than household belongings westward. Lucy took with her a mother’s counsel and a hope in God that would become part of the spiritual life of her own children.

Autumn 1823

Gathering to listen

Lucy remembered the family drawing together in the evenings to listen as Joseph spoke about what he had learned. Parents, older siblings and younger children shared the room and gave their attention to him.

These conversations belonged to family life before the Church was organized. Listening, asking and returning to the conversation together helped them face changes whose full meaning they could not yet see.

November 1823

Alvin’s charge to his family

As Alvin approached death, he spoke to his brothers and sisters about the responsibilities they would carry after him. He asked Hyrum to finish the house for their parents, encouraged Sophronia to be kind to them, and urged Joseph to remain faithful and set a good example for the younger children.

Alvin died on 19 November 1823, at twenty-five. His counsel joined faith to practical duties: finish the work, care for your parents and help those who are younger than you.

21–22 September 1827

Waiting through the night

After Joseph asked about a chest with a lock and left with Emma, Lucy waited through the night in prayer. Morning brought the familiar work of preparing breakfast, but her thoughts remained with her son.

Her account lets us pause with the mother who waited while the events of the Restoration unfolded beyond her sight. Prayer and daily work stood beside one another in her care for Joseph.

Late September 1827

Hyrum comes when he is needed

When Don Carlos brought the message that Joseph needed a chest, Hyrum emptied one and carried it to him. Lucy’s history gives this practical act a place in the story of protecting the plates.

We can see Hyrum’s loyalty in the weight he was willing to carry. He responded to his brother’s need with something useful, and he went without delay.

Summer 1828

A mother speaks of mercy

After Martin Harris reported the manuscript missing, Joseph was overwhelmed with grief. Lucy tried to comfort him with the hope that repentance could bring forgiveness.

She did not make the loss small. She helped her son look beyond despair toward God’s mercy. In this moment, the family story invites us to consider how we speak to someone who knows that a serious mistake has been made.

6 April 1830

A son’s joy at his father’s baptism

Lucy remembered Joseph’s tears of joy after his father was baptized. He leaned against his father and wept, grateful to see him take this step of faith.

The Restoration was also a family’s religious journey. For Joseph, this was a moment to hold his father close and rejoice in a commitment to Jesus Christ.

Winter 1840–1841

The care comes home

After Joseph Sr.’s death, Lucy injured her knee and became ill. She remembered weeks in which family members kept watch through the nights, and she named the devoted care of Sophronia, Arthur Millikin and her youngest daughter, Lucy.

The mother who had watched beside sick children now received their care. Here the family story brings us back to the love that crosses generations, and to Lucy’s gratitude for the people who stayed near through a difficult season.

The families Joseph and Emma called home

Before they made a home together, Joseph and Emma belonged to two large families. Their parents, brothers and sisters shaped the places and relationships from which their married life began. Their own children brought both joy and deep sorrow.

Follow the two families separately, then the children Joseph and Emma raised and remembered. Each name belongs to a different lifetime; these records let us hold those lives together without losing their dates.

Joseph’s parents, brothers and sisters

Joseph Smith Sr. (12 July 1771–14 September 1840) and Lucy Mack Smith (8 July 1775–14 May 1856) married on 24 January 1796. Their eleven children included Joseph, his older brother Hyrum, and two sons who died in infancy.

The names also reveal how closely loss touched this family. Alvin died in 1823. Don Carlos died in 1841. Joseph and Hyrum died on the same day in June 1844, and Samuel died the following month. Their mother, Lucy Mack Smith, lived until 1856.

Emma’s parents, brothers and sisters

Isaac Hale (21 March 1763–11 January 1839) and Elizabeth Lewis Hale (19 November 1767–16 February 1842) married on 20 September 1790. Emma was the seventh of their nine children.

Their home stood in Pennsylvania’s Susquehanna Valley. Emma’s childhood began in the family’s log home; the later frame house was built around 1809. When Joseph and Emma moved to Harmony in 1827, they returned to the region and relationships she had known growing up.

Joseph and Emma’s children

Joseph and Emma’s family included nine children born to Emma and the two Murdock twins they adopted. In 1831, Emma’s newborn twins died. John Murdock’s wife, Julia Clapp, died after giving birth to twins that same day; Joseph and Emma welcomed those children, Julia and Joseph, into their home.

Several of their children died very young. Julia, Joseph III, Frederick, Alexander and David Hyrum lived into adulthood. David was born in November 1844, after his father’s death. Their family story therefore continues beyond the years Joseph and Emma shared.

18 January 1827 · Joseph 21, Emma 22

Choosing a life together

Joseph and Emma married in South Bainbridge, New York, despite opposition from Emma’s family, then moved to his parents’ home. Their partnership began before the translation and publication of the Book of Mormon.

Consider: What does it mean to begin a shared life without knowing all it will require?

Early December 1827 · Joseph 21, Emma 23

Making a new home

Joseph and Emma arrived in Pennsylvania’s Susquehanna Valley in early December 1827. They moved toward Emma’s family while seeking a place to live, work and continue the translation. Historical accounts also credit her brother Alva with helping the move.

Emma was expecting their first child when they arrived in Pennsylvania.

Consider: What help makes a difficult move or new beginning possible?

Harmony · early 1828

Emma’s work as a scribe

Emma helped Joseph as an early scribe. Martin Harris took over that role in April 1828. Her writing belongs within the history of the translation, as well as the history of their marriage.

Emma was expecting their first child during this early scribal work.

Consider: Whose quiet work helps make a larger undertaking possible?

Harmony · June 1828

Staying close in sorrow

Their first child died shortly after his birth on 15 June 1828. Emma became seriously ill, and Joseph cared for her through the following two weeks.

Consider: How can steady presence help when words cannot remove another person’s grief?

Harmony · autumn 1828

Work that sustained a household

Joseph supported the household through hired farm labor, including hauling wood. The ledger places this work alongside the demands of translation and family life.

Consider: How can necessary everyday work become an expression of love?

Harmony · December 4, 1828

A small gift amid demanding work

Joseph earned three dollars for four days of work and spent twenty-five cents on a comb for Emma. A modest purchase brings their household concerns into view.

Consider: Which small act of thoughtfulness could express care in your home?

Late June 1830 · Emma 25

Praying together through uncertainty

Emma was baptized on 28 June 1830, but Joseph’s arrest disrupted the planned confirmation meeting. While he faced custody and court proceedings, she joined other women in prayer. Their shared worship offered a way to turn toward God amid anxiety.

Consider: Who could you join in prayer when someone is facing uncertainty?

November 1838 · Emma 34

Love carried across separation

On 12 November 1838, Joseph wrote to Emma while held in Richmond, Missouri. He expressed affection for her, addressed their children, and hoped for their reunion. His invitation to visit left room for Emma’s own judgment. The letter makes the family’s separation personal.

Their youngest living child, Alexander, had been born that June. Concern for the children formed part of the separation these letters sought to bridge.

Consider: What can a thoughtful letter offer someone you cannot be with?

Nauvoo · 17 March 1842

Seeking out those in need

At the first meeting of the Female Relief Society of Nauvoo, Emma was elected president. After the men withdrew, she urged the women to find and relieve people in distress, do good, and care for one another’s character and reputation.

She undertook this service about six weeks after giving birth to a son who did not survive.

Consider: Who nearby needs to be noticed, treated fairly, or helped?

Joseph and Hyrum

Beside my brother

Joseph and Hyrum shared the work and trials of the Restoration. Their relationship also belonged to the daily life of a family: two brothers whose affection and loyalty endured through difficulty.

Consider: How can affection and loyalty strengthen the relationships closest to you?

June 1844 · Hyrum 44

Hyrum reflects beside scripture

Doctrine and Covenants 135 remembers Hyrum reading near the close of Ether 12 on the morning he prepared to leave for Carthage. He marked the passage by turning down the page. Its words of charity, faithfulness and strength in weakness place his final journey within his devotion to Jesus Christ.

Consider: What passage of scripture helps you remain faithful when the way ahead is uncertain?

Forgiveness and remembrance

In July 1840, William W. Phelps sought reconciliation after his break with the Church. Joseph’s reply welcomed his return and expressed joy in renewed friendship.

After Joseph and Hyrum were killed, Phelps’s poem, now known as “Praise to the Man,” appeared in the Times and Seasons on 1 August 1844. It joined remembrance of Joseph’s ministry with hope in his work beyond death.

A study you can carry into the week

These focusChrist prompts are invitations for personal reflection.

First session: establish the question

Choose a single question about Joseph's life. Write it in language that a source could help answer: what did he report, when was it recorded, or how did a particular teaching develop? Avoid making one question stand for his entire life.

Second session: follow a citation

Open a document linked from the First Vision essay. Read the surrounding passage. Note the recorder, recording date, and what the document actually says. If your question concerns a different period, use the Church History section to find the relevant sources.

Third session: assess the interpretation

Write a short account of what you learned, separating evidence from your explanation of it. Name any remaining uncertainty. A source can establish what a witness reported without resolving every question about the experience being described.

Fourth session: consider the spiritual claim

Read D&C 6 with the historical questions still clearly named. What does its invitation to seek wisdom, accept correction, and look to Christ mean for your own conduct? Personal reflection can accompany careful investigation without replacing it.
