The Plan of Salvation

Where we came from, why this life matters, and the hope Jesus Christ makes possible.

Continue↓

Some questions reach into every part of life: Who am I? Why are we here? What happens when someone dies? Latter-day Saints look to the Father's plan of salvation for answers, with Jesus Christ at its center.

You can follow the whole journey or begin with the question closest to you. The scriptures do not answer every detail, but they teach why your life matters and why there is reason to turn toward Christ.

You belong to God's family

A life that begins before birth

A new child can make familiar questions feel very close: Who is this person? What will this life hold? The scriptures begin our story before that first breath. Abraham learned that human life had a history before the earth was made. The Church teaches that we lived with our heavenly parents as Their spirit children.

That teaching gives every person a worth that does not depend on usefulness, achievement or recognition. We arrive here to receive a body and learn to choose, love and grow. We do not remember that earlier life, and we cannot read someone's mortal circumstances as a measure of their premortal worth.

The book of Moses also places Jesus Christ at the beginning of this story. The Beloved Son accepts the Father’s will, while Satan seeks to take away human agency. Our hope rests in the Savior who honors the Father and makes a willing response possible.

Abraham 3:22–26 Plan of Salvation Moses 4:1–4

More than getting through the day

Moses beholds the earth and its inhabitants by the Spirit, then asks why these things exist. The Lord answers with a purpose that reaches to every person: immortality and eternal life. Through Jesus Christ, all will rise from the dead. Eternal life means life with God, made possible through the Savior.

It can be hard to think beyond the next responsibility. Yet purpose need not make ordinary work feel small. Caring for a person, learning patience and beginning again after a mistake can all belong to a life turned toward God. The destination helps us decide how to live today.

Moses 1:27–40 Moses 1:39 Preach My Gospel, lesson 2

An earth prepared for us

An earth prepared for life

Moses describes the earth taking shape through the Only Begotten. Dry land appears, and plants bear seed and fruit. The account directs our attention to a world that can sustain life and to the divine purpose behind it.

The Creation belongs at the beginning of this study because bodies and earthly experience matter. Food, shelter and the living world are part of our mortal home. Looking closely at a leaf or a piece of fruit can be a simple way to remember how much we receive before we have done anything to earn it.

Moses 2:1, 9–12

Pause and consider

What part of the created world helps you feel gratitude?

Learning to care for what we receive

Genesis describes women and men in the image of God and gives them responsibility within the living world. Human dignity and responsibility appear together. The earth is a gift, and our use of it affects other people and other forms of life.

A child learning to tend a young tree can discover something simple: growth needs care. We can receive the world gratefully while also protecting what another generation will need. Our place in the Creation invites attention rather than indifference.

Genesis 1:26–31

Learning to choose

Adam and Eve begin life outside Eden

After leaving Eden, Adam tills the earth, and Eve works with him. Moses places them together in the labor of mortal life. They have children, call upon God and learn that redemption will come through His Son.

Their story includes loss, learning and hope. Eve speaks of knowing good and evil and of the joy of redemption. The Fall opens the way to mortal experience; Christ opens the way through its sin and death. We do not have to blame Eve or dismiss the cost of mortality to recognize both truths.

Moses 5:1–12

Lehi teaches Jacob about freedom

Lehi speaks to Jacob as a father who knows that his son has suffered. He does not pretend that those early hurts were good. He turns Jacob toward the Redeemer and teaches how opposition, choice and redemption belong to mortal life.

Lehi's invitation is to choose liberty and eternal life through the Messiah. Agency is more than being left alone with consequences. Christ makes repentance and a different future possible. We can take responsibility for our choices without calling another person's harm our fault.

2 Nephi 2:1–3, 22–29

Living in a mortal body

A body worth caring for

A body lets us work, learn, rest and reach toward someone we love. It also makes us vulnerable. Illness and disability can change the shape of a day or a life, but they do not lessen the worth of the person living it.

When the disciples asked whose sin had caused a man's blindness, Jesus rejected their proposed blame. Alma teaches that Christ knows His people's pains and infirmities. Those passages can lead us toward practical compassion: listening, seeking appropriate care and making room for a person's own voice.

John 9:1–7 Alma 7:11–13

Corianton receives a way forward

Alma's teaching about the plan comes in a conversation with his son Corianton. He addresses wrongdoing seriously, then explains mercy through the Atonement. His purpose is to bring his son to repentance rather than leave him trapped in excuses or despair.

At the end, Alma sends Corianton to teach truth and help others repent. A changed life still has work to do. For us, returning to Christ may include confessing, repairing what we can and accepting help as we learn to live differently.

Alma 42:23–31

Jesus Christ makes the way

The promised Savior bears our griefs

Isaiah describes a servant acquainted with grief who bears the burdens of others. Christians read this passage in the light of Jesus Christ. His suffering is not distant from the pain people carry; it belongs at the center of the promise of redemption.

Alma adds that the Savior will take upon Himself sickness, infirmity, death and sin. These are not all the same experience, and people need more than one kind of help. We can seek forgiveness for wrongdoing and seek comfort or care for suffering we did not cause.

Isaiah 53:3–6 Alma 7:11–13

Jesus prays for eternal life

Jesus lifts His eyes to heaven and prays to the Father. He speaks of the glory He had before the world and of the work He has done on earth. The same prayer names eternal life as knowing the Father and Jesus Christ.

Before His suffering, the Savior's prayer reaches beyond those immediately around Him to people who will believe through their witness. The plan is personal: a relationship with the Father and Son, made possible through Christ. His prayer gives us more to seek than simply a life without difficulty.

John 17:1–5, 20–23 Doctrine and Covenants 19:16–19

Following Christ together

A covenant reaches toward other people

At the waters of Mormon, Alma connects baptism with a willingness to bear burdens, mourn with those who mourn and stand as a witness of God. A covenant is a committed relationship with God, and it changes how we approach His children.

Later in the chapter, people share what they have according to their ability and others' needs. Someone who has little is not required to pretend to have much. The invitation is willing care, shaped by the real needs and capacities of a community.

Nephi describes the way into this life of discipleship through faith in Christ, repentance, baptism and the gift of the Holy Ghost. Then we continue with hope, love and trust in the Savior. Covenants are not a substitute for His grace; they commit us to follow Him and receive the help He offers.

Mosiah 18:8–10, 27–29 2 Nephi 31:17–21

Children have a place in Christ's ministry

Third Nephi records that Jesus ministered to children and enabled them to speak marvelous things to their fathers. The record does not give us their words. It does show that children were participants in His ministry, not merely background to the adults' experience.

The chapter goes on to describe people teaching, serving and dealing justly with one another. Learning Christ's gospel can change both whom we listen to and how we live together. A child, a quiet neighbor or a new learner may deserve more patient attention than we first realize.

3 Nephi 26:13–20

Life continues beyond death

Death is not the end of a person

Alma distinguishes death from resurrection. Between them, the spirit continues to live. He describes peace for the righteous and also the painful condition of those who have chosen wickedness. This is an intermediate state, not the final resurrection or a complete map of eternity.

For someone grieving, those teachings may sit beside longing and unanswered questions. Faith does not require us to stop missing a person. We can speak honestly about loss while holding to Christ's promise that death will not have the last word.

Alma 40:6–14, 21–23

Pause and consider

What would help you remember someone with both honesty and hope?

The gospel reaches beyond death

Joseph F. Smith's vision begins as he ponders the scriptures and the Savior's redeeming sacrifice. He learns that Christ organized messengers to carry the gospel to the dead. Those who lacked its knowledge are not simply forgotten.

The revelation names faith, repentance and ordinances performed on behalf of the dead. It preserves a willing response: redemption is not forced on anyone. Family history and temple service can therefore be acts of love for people whose lives continue beyond our sight.

Doctrine and Covenants 138:1–11, 29–35, 58–59

Raised and known by Christ

Christ makes resurrection possible

Paul teaches the Resurrection in letters and in public. In Athens he speaks of the Creator, our relationship to God and the risen One through whom God will judge the world. In his letter to Corinth, he makes the promise clear: as death reaches all through Adam, life through Christ will reach all as well.

Paul also compares the body laid down in death with a seed that grows into a different form. The comparison helps him teach transformation without making the body worthless. Our final hope rests in what Christ has done, even while many details of that future remain beyond our experience.

1 Corinthians 15:20–26, 35–44 Alma 40:6–14, 21–23 Acts 17:22–31

A judgment that knows the whole person

Jesus teaches that the Father has entrusted judgment to the Son. The One who judges is also the One who gives life. His judgment is just; we do not have to turn our incomplete understanding of someone into a final verdict.

Joseph Smith's vision of Alvin teaches that the Lord considers both works and the desires of the heart, including what people would have received had they known the gospel. Children who die before accountability are also included in the promise of celestial life. These truths leave room for both accountability and profound trust.

John 5:22–30 Doctrine and Covenants 137:5–10

Receiving God's glory

God offers more than a narrow either-or

Doctrine and Covenants 76 describes celestial, terrestrial and telestial glory. These are not three stages of mortal life, and they are not labels for sorting the people around us. The revelation expands our understanding of God's mercy while taking our response to Christ seriously.

President Dallin H. Oaks teaches that God's plan offers His children kingdoms of glory and invites them to receive the fulness of life with Him. The highest hope is not an achievement we manufacture alone. The revelation describes people made perfect through Jesus Christ.

Doctrine and Covenants 76:50–70, 71–81, 89–92 Kingdoms of Glory: Dallin H. Oaks, October 2023

Eternal life is life with God

Eternal life means more than living for a very long time. Jesus describes it as knowing the Father and the Son. The celestial promise includes dwelling in Their presence, with lives changed through the Savior.

Covenants teach us to love and remain faithful as we move toward that promise. We should not use another person's present family circumstances to declare what God will withhold from them. We can seek the blessings He offers and trust Him with what we cannot yet see.

Living the plan today

Bring eternity into one decision

President Russell M. Nelson invites us to take an eternal view of our choices and put Jesus Christ first. That counsel can reach into very ordinary decisions: how we respond to frustration, what we give our attention to and whether we seek help when we need it.

You do not have to change every part of life at once to take a sincere step. Choose something specific enough to practice. A difficult apology, a needed conversation or time set aside for prayer may matter more today than an elaborate plan that never becomes an action.

Think Celestial! (Russell M. Nelson, October 2023)

Pause and consider

What is one decision you want to make with Christ in mind?

A king asks what he can do

Aaron teaches Lamoni’s father from the scriptures, beginning with Creation and the Fall and continuing to redemption through Christ. The king responds with a personal question: what must he do to receive eternal life? He is willing to give up his possessions, but Aaron directs him toward faith, repentance and prayer.

The king kneels and asks God to make Himself known, offering to give up his sins. The story brings this whole journey back to a willing heart. You may still have questions about the plan. You can keep learning while choosing one honest step toward Christ today.

Alma 22:12–18
