Faith in Trials

How Can Faith in Jesus Christ Help During Trials?

Continue↓
Answers / Faith and adversity

Latter-day Saint teaching does not promise that faith will remove every trial. It teaches that trusting Jesus Christ can give strength to endure, wisdom to act, power to change, and hope that suffering is not the final word.

Spiritual support can coexist with practical help. Serious mental-health, medical, safety, legal, or abuse-related problems may require qualified professional or emergency assistance in addition to prayer and faith.

Faith Can Include Honest Sorrow

Faith in Jesus Christ includes belief in Him, but it goes further: it means relying on His power, wisdom, and love. A faithful person may still grieve, fear, question, or feel exhausted. Scripture is full of disciples who suffered while continuing to turn toward God.

Christ Understands Suffering

Latter-day Saints emphasize scriptural teachings that Jesus Christ took upon Himself pains, sicknesses, temptations, grief, and sin. Because of His Atonement, believers trust that He understands mortal suffering personally and can strengthen people in ways that are both spiritual and practical.

Read Alma 7:11-13 slowly. Alma speaks of suffering, sickness, infirmity, death, and sin. He connects the Savior's experience of mortality with His knowledge of how to help His people. This passage gives a reason to bring Him the particular pain you carry, even when you cannot explain it neatly.

Trials Have Different Causes

Church teaching does not reduce every hardship to punishment from God. Some consequences follow personal choices; some suffering results from the choices of others; some comes through illness, accident, mortality, injustice, or conditions no individual caused. Different problems therefore require different responses.

Taking One Step When Life Is Hard

It is understandable to ask why, and you do not have to turn pain into a lesson before seeking help. When you have the capacity, consider one small question: Who could listen today? What practical need could someone help with? What might help me get through this afternoon? A next step can be small, and accepting help can be part of it.

Hope Reaches Beyond the Present Moment

Latter-day Saint hope is ultimately tied to Christ’s Resurrection. Loss, disability, injustice, and death are real, but they are not eternal victories over God’s children. Resurrection, judgment, healing, and eternal life place present suffering inside a larger story.

When the Question Is Personal

If your trial involves loss or changes in family life, you may find more specific support in Finding Hope After the Death of a Child, Divorce and Faith, or Prayer and Personal Revelation. Choose the subject that fits what you are carrying; there is no need to work through everything at once.

Receiving Help Is Part of Being Cared For

You may need a listening friend, help with daily responsibilities, or care from a qualified professional. It can be difficult to name a need when you are worn down. You might begin with something specific: asking someone to bring a meal, accompany you to an appointment, or check in tomorrow.

In Mosiah 18:8-10, those preparing for baptism commit to share burdens and comfort people who need comfort. This gives friends and congregations something concrete to do. The person suffering should not have to carry the whole responsibility for finding support.

  • Like a Broken Vessel: Jeffrey R. Holland addresses mental and emotional suffering, including the importance of qualified care and compassion. Read or watch the talk if that is the kind of support you are seeking.
  • Grief and Loss: guidance for supporting someone through loss, with attention to listening and differing experiences of grief.

Read Suffering Without Simplifying It

A prayer from distress

Doctrine and Covenants 121:1-9 begins with Joseph Smith pleading from imprisonment and then records words of comfort. Notice that the anguished question is part of the scripture. Do not rush past it to reach a reassuring line. A personal prayer can be honest about distress without becoming a polished lesson.

Help before circumstances change

Follow Mosiah 24:10-21 through the whole sequence. The people cry to God, face threats for praying aloud, and continue praying in their hearts. The Lord strengthens them under their burdens; later, He leads them out of bondage. The account includes help within hardship and deliverance from it. Their story does not establish when another person's circumstances will change.

Hope when endurance feels small

In Mountains to Climb, President Henry B. Eyring addresses people who fear that their faith is weakening under pressure. He describes a foundation built through patient acts of integrity and service. He also acknowledges that he cannot promise an end to adversity in this life. Listen for encouragement that fits your capacity today, with room for the sorrow you still carry.

In Matthew 14:27-33, Peter's fear is followed by a cry for help and Christ's immediate reach. Read the rescue as carefully as the question Jesus asks him. The Savior remains central throughout the encounter.

A personal reflection: Choose whichever question fits today: What do I wish someone understood? What help could I receive? What can I bring honestly to prayer? You can return to another question later, without a schedule or requirement to feel stronger first.

Read for compassion, not comparison

A story of endurance can encourage one person and feel heavy to another. Avoid comparing your capacity with a scriptural figure or treating someone's continuing suffering as proof of weak faith. Ask what the account actually says, what it does not promise, and what practical help is needed in your circumstances.

What would support look like this week?

Name a need that someone else could reasonably help meet. It might be a conversation, transport, childcare, or an appointment. You can pair that request with prayer without waiting to feel certain or strong.

A quiet reflection for the strength you have today

Mosiah 24:13–15 describes divine help while burdens remain. If you wish, choose the question that fits today and leave the others for another time.

What do you wish someone understood?

You may name a feeling, a practical difficulty, or something you cannot yet explain. What words would help a trusted person listen? Writing a sentence or asking someone to sit with you can be enough for this moment.

What help would make the next step possible?

Think about the next few hours rather than the whole future. Is there a task, appointment, meal, or conversation someone could share? You can receive care while the larger question remains unanswered.

What can you bring honestly to Christ?

Return to the passage on this page that feels closest to your experience. What would you say in prayer if you did not have to hide sorrow or sound strong? Allow the reflection to end where your capacity ends today.
